Section 01 · what this is

Product Overview

A private map of the places you chose, built from links you already save, organised by where those places are.

You paste one link. The app reads the post, finds the places the caption names, gives each one real coordinates and a real identity, and shows you what it found. You confirm what you want. It lands on your map, with the video that convinced you always one tap away.

Everything else in this document follows from that exchange being small enough to be worth making. The product is not trying to be a better place to keep things. It is trying to make the things you already kept reachable at the one moment they are worth anything — when you are standing somewhere, deciding where to go.

One paste

The whole of the user's side of the exchange

TikTok

The one source we read today, and we say so up front

Private

No feed, no followers, nothing public to set up

Yours

Only what can be kept forever is stored

What it does, end to end

A signed-in person pastes a TikTok link into one field. The post is read server-side. The caption is examined for the places it names. Each candidate is matched against a places directory so it gains real coordinates and a real identity.

Nothing is written until the person confirms. What they confirm lands on their map as pins — one pin per place, however many videos pointed at it — each keeping a permanent link back to the post that recommended it.

The map is not a feature of this product. The map is how you get things back.

Location replaces search as the way a person finds their own saved places. Every other decision in the product — what is stored, what is shown, what is refused — is downstream of the map being the retrieval interface rather than a view of a list.

The boundary we hold, stated up front

We read what a post says in words. A great many TikTok videos name the place only on screen or only out loud, and those we cannot read. So a link that yields no place is not a breakage — it is an ordinary result, it has its own designed screen, and it offers three ways forward.

What we will never do is ask you to type the caption in. The moment the product asks a person for text they could have typed into any notes app, it has stopped being worth having. The boundary is narrow on purpose, and holding it is what keeps the promise honest.

Where this sits

The category is crowded. Turning a social link into a pin on a personal map is a well-populated idea, and No Crumbs does not win on having more of it. Three things are defended instead.

Honesty about what the app worked out

What the post said, what the app inferred, and what you wrote stay separable. An uncertain result is shown as uncertain, never dressed up as settled.

The common failure is designed, not patched

The result where nothing is found has its own screen, its own words and its own ways forward — because it is a result, not an accident.

The library is genuinely yours

Only what can be kept forever is stored, and privacy is enforced where the data lives rather than by each screen remembering to check.

Section 02 · the problem

Problem Statement & Business Value

Collecting works perfectly. Getting it back is broken — and getting it back is the only moment that matters.

Recommendations for real places — restaurants, cafés, bars, viewpoints, shops — increasingly arrive as social video. The natural response is to save the post. TikTok stores that save as a video, in the order you saved it. It does not store where the place is, what kind of place it is, or what it is called.

So the collecting half works and the retrieval half does not. Every question a person actually asks of a saved collection is a question about location:

  1. I'm in this neighbourhood right now — what did I save near me?
  2. I land in Tokyo on Thursday — what do I already have there?
  3. I want dinner tonight and I don't want to scroll.

A thumbnail in a saved folder rarely shows the name or the city, so answering any of those means opening posts one at a time and reading them — work you do standing up, at the worst possible moment, with a decision waiting on it.

Turning a post into a place is work the platform has no reason to do. Today the person does it in their own head, from a grid of thumbnails, at exactly the wrong time.

Why this is worth building rather than tolerating

The expensive half is already paid for

People have done the hard part — the scrolling, the judgement, the decision that a place is worth remembering. This is not a new habit to learn. It is a collection they already have, unlocked.

What comes out lasts longer than what went in

A real name, real coordinates, a category, your note and a link back — organised by location, in your own account, not dependent on a saved folder keeping its current design or its current mind.

The cost to the user is a single paste

Every competing way to solve this asks a person to type something. That is the difference between a product and a chore.

The decision improves, not just the search

A collection that cannot be reached at the moment of choosing has an effective value of zero, whatever its size. Reaching it is the entire return.

One clause we keep honest

What you keep is yours — the name, the category, the coordinates, your note and your Been marks all survive independently of the platform. The link back to the original post is the exception: it works for as long as TikTok continues to serve that post. We say so rather than implying otherwise.

Section 03 · who it is for

Target Audience

One person with two retrieval questions — and no second party buying access to them.

Everyday

“What did I save around here?”

The trigger. Standing in a neighbourhood, deciding where to eat.

How it is answered. Places near you, nearest first, offered on permission and never demanded.

Trips

“What do I already have in this city?”

The trigger. Planning a trip, or landing somewhere.

How it is answered. The whole library on one map, anywhere in the world, with no location permission needed.

This is one profile, not two segments. The same person saves places in the city they live in and banks places for a trip they have not taken yet. Designing for two audiences here would produce two half-products.

Two facts that shape every decision in the product

01

This happens on a phone, standing up, mid-scroll

Mobile is not the smaller version of this product; it is the product. Sheets you can drag, actions inside thumb reach, nothing important stranded at the top of a tall screen. Desktop is supported and secondary.

02

Adding is the cost; finding is the reward

Every tap it takes to add a place is paid before any value arrives. That is why the quality budget is spent there, and why the person's side of the exchange is held to exactly one paste.

Who this is not for

Stated plainly, so the boundary can be argued with.

  • People looking for somewhere new. There is no feed, no ranking, no “places you might like”. No Crumbs never suggests a place you did not save.
  • Trip planners who want day-by-day routing, bookings or opening hours. Different product, different data.
  • Creators and businesses who want an audience. There is no follower graph and no public profile.
  • Anyone with a handful of places. Below a certain size a plain list beats a map. The product is worth nothing at zero places — which is a real constraint on the first session, not a footnote.

The user and the customer are the same person

There is no advertiser, no business account, and nobody buying access to anyone's data or attention. The product's only obligation is to the person whose map it is.

That is not a positioning flourish — it is why privacy works the way it does. A private map, ownership enforced where the data lives, and your live location never kept on our side.

No price is proposed and nothing in this version was built to make money. If it ever were, the natural direction is a paid tier for the heaviest savers, because they feel the problem most — but that is a direction, not a plan, and nobody has been asked to pay for anything.

What we defend instead of feature count

  • Honesty about what the app worked out. Said, inferred and yours stay separable.
  • The common failure is designed. Finding nothing has its own screen, not an error message.
  • Ownership is real. Only what can be kept forever is stored, and permission is enforced where the data lives.

Goal 5 carries the other four. If the product asked you to type in what the post said, it would be a manual map with extra steps — and goals 1 to 4 are things a notes app already gives you. Every scope decision in this product exists to keep the user's side of the exchange down to one paste.

What success looks like

Not “the search was faster”. The outcome that matters is the saved recommendation was actually used — that the place someone kept months ago is the place they walk into tonight.

Adding and finding are separated cleanly. Adding is a batch activity — a person clears a backlog in one sitting. Finding is an in-the-moment activity. Each gets its own surface instead of compromising to share one.

The first session ends with places on the map. The product is worthless empty, so onboarding is not education — it is getting the first few places in.

What we store, and why that is a business decision

Everything kept is restricted to what can be held forever: name, category, coordinates, the link to the post, and your own note. No opening hours, no photos, no ratings, no reviews — those come with expiry dates attached, and a library assembled from them is rented, not owned. That restriction is the reason goal 4 can be said out loud.

Three guarantees behind the set

Privacy is enforced where the data lives

Not by each screen remembering to check. Your map is private because the data itself refuses to be read by anyone else, which is a stronger promise than a policy page.

A post can never tell the app what to do

What a caption says is treated as words to read, never as instructions to follow. The content we ingest is somebody else's, and it is handled that way throughout.

Your notes stay yours, even when you share

People you share a collection with see the places, their categories and who added what. They do not see your note, your tags, whether you have Been, or which video you saved it from.

What the product deliberately cannot do

gap

Read audio or on-screen text

The single change that would most widen what the product can read. Deferred on cost and speed, not refused on principle.

gap

Export your library

No export surface exists. For a version with real users this is the most obvious thing missing from an ownership claim, and it is named rather than hidden.

gap

Keep a place it cannot map

A post can name something we cannot place. Today that mention is not kept, which means a small amount of what a caption offered is lost rather than parked.

gap

Work offline, or appear in the phone's share sheet

A web app cannot enter the native share sheet. Designing a flow that cannot exist is worse than not having one.

Section 06 · how it is used

Core User Flows

Six flows, in the order a person meets them. Three are drawn, because their shape is the argument; three are sequential and are told as steps.

01 · Your first places

The product is worth nothing at zero places, so the first session has exactly one job: end with places on the map.

  1. A visitor lands on a page that says what this does in three steps — paste a TikTok link → check what we found → it lands on your map — and states the boundary right there: works with TikTok links today.
  2. They create an account. It is private from the first second; there is nothing public to set up.
  3. The first thing they see is an empty map, and it is designed rather than blank: one line, one clear action, and a map already looking at a sensible part of the world rather than at the middle of the ocean.

The empty state is one line and one button, and the restraint is deliberate: no checklist, no progress meter, no illustration, no permission prompt. The one action is the one that matters.

Add a TikTok link

The single button on the empty map. The screen it opens carries real example videos, for someone who has nothing of their own to hand yet.

Add a place by name

Available from the create menu on any tab, for the person who already knows where they want to start and does not need a post at all.

The bar for this flow is not comprehension. It is places on the map before the person closes the tab.

02 · Add a TikTok link

The flagship. Eight stages, four places it can legitimately diverge, and one genuine hard stop.

  1. Paste. Copy a link in TikTok, come back, paste it into the one field. Any shape works.
  2. It is checked before anything is sent. A link that is not a TikTok video is recognised immediately and handed to adding by name, so a wrong link costs nothing.
  3. See the post. The post you just pasted appears almost immediately — its picture, the handle and its caption — while the slower work runs underneath. You are looking at something the whole time.
  4. The caption is read for the places it names. Finding none is a legitimate answer, and it leads to its own screen rather than to an error.
  5. Each name is matched to a real place, so it gains coordinates and an identity. A name alone is not something you can put on a map.
  6. Review, with nothing saved yet. One card per place. Confident matches arrive ticked; uncertain ones arrive as a choice. You can change any of it, add a note, and untick anything you do not want.
  7. Confirm. The places land on your map, one pin per place, each keeping its link back to the post. If some could not be saved, you are told how many — nothing is quietly dropped.
  8. Find it again, weeks later. This is the step the other seven exist for.

03 · When a post names no place

Not an error. A screen with its own words, its own picture and its own ways forward — and the one this product shows most often.

It tells the truth about which case it is, because these are not the same problem and do not have the same answer. The last row is the case that goes somewhere else entirely.

What happenedWhat you are told
The post has no caption at allThere is no caption on this one — the post's boundary, not yours
The caption named nothing we can put on a mapNo places in this one
The caption named a city or an area, but no place in itThe same, and the city is used to narrow the search offered below
Places were found but none could be savedA different screen, with what to do about it

Two ways forward, strongest first. Add it by name — you watched the video, you know the place; type the name, pick it from what comes back, done, in fewer steps than the flow you just came from. Or tell us what you remember — a single sentence, folded into the results, which carries the detail a bare name loses and lets someone offer a name they are not sure is a name.

A third route is always there: open the original post.

What never appears anywhere in this flow: a request to type the caption in.

Adding a place by name also stands on its own. From the create menu on any tab, one field takes either a link or a name — and what you typed decides what happens, without you having to choose a mode first.

  1. For the place you already know. Type a name, pick the right one, and it is on your map. No post required.
  2. Two ways in, deliberately different. From the create menu, where you are typing a name you are sure of, it saves in one shot. From the no-places screen, where you are recovering, it shows you what it found and asks you to pick — and links the save to the post that prompted it.
  3. Typing something you already saved finds it instead of duplicating it. “I already have this” is the more likely intention than “make me a second one”.
  4. It lands exactly like a place added from a post — same pin, same detail, same map — with its origin recorded honestly.

04 · Find it again

The payoff. It is listed fourth because it happens on the second day, not because it matters least — everything above is the price of this.

  1. You open your map and your places are already on it.
  2. It opens framed around what you have, so you are looking at somewhere useful straight away rather than at a default point on the globe.
  3. Zoom out for countries, in for areas with names and counts, in again for individual pins, coloured by what kind of place they are.
  4. Near you, offered rather than demanded, moves the map to what you have around you, nearest first.
  5. Or use the list beside it — search a name, filter by category, tap a tag, hide the ones you have already Been to.
  6. Or type what you want as a sentence and let it set the filters for you.
  7. Tap a pin and the whole record is there, including the TikTok video that made you save it in the first place.

05 · Share a collection

A collection is your map with a different set of pins — which is why it did not widen what this product is.

  1. Make a collection, name it, and put places from your library in it.
  2. Generate a link, and choose whether the person joins as an editor or a viewer. There is only ever one live invite per collection; making a new one plainly retires the last.
  3. Before you share, the access fact is stated outright — anyone with the link who signs in can open this collection. The detail of which fields they see and which stay yours sits one tap away, under a control that names what is behind it, and the two halves are always shown together so neither can be read without the other.
  4. They open the link. Signed out, they get a real explanation of what a collection is before being asked to make an account. Signed in, they see who invited them and what they will be able to do.
  5. Someone joining is asked what to call them first, because joining makes them visible to other people as who added what.
  6. Both people now see the collection on their own map. A viewer can copy a shared place into their own library.

The disclosure, stated at the moment of the decision rather than buried in a settings page.

Where you intend to go in future is a different thing from a restaurant recommendation, and it is not shared.

06 · Leave, and take nothing with you by accident

Rules rather than a flow. The interesting part is the one case where deleting is refused, and the one thing that survives.

  1. Delete my data removes the account and everything in it. One action, no negotiation.
  2. Deleting one place is always available, separately, whenever you want it.

It refuses, and explains, when deleting would take something away from other people

If you own a collection other people are in, deletion stops and names those collections, with a way to go and sort it out. A refusal that tells you what to do is a different thing from a refusal.

One thing survives, and the product says so

A note you wrote inside someone else's shared collection stays with them, with your name taken off it. No screen claims everything you wrote is gone.

Where this document ends is a limit, not a flourish

Two things a reader will look for and not find. The product reads words, not audio or on-screen text, which is the single change that would most widen what it can do. And there is no way to export your library, which is the most obvious thing missing from a product that says what you keep is yours. Both are named here rather than left to be discovered.
