Section 01 · what this is

Product Overview

No Crumbs is a private map of the places you chose, built from links you already save.

You paste one link. The app reads the post, finds the places the caption names, gives each one real coordinates and a real identity, and shows you what it found. You confirm what you want. It lands on your map, with the video that convinced you one tap away.

The map is not one of the features; it is how you get things back. You reach your own saved places by where they are, not by searching for them — and what we store, what we show, and what we refuse all follow from that one decision.

The boundary we hold, stated once

We read what a post says in words. A great many TikTok videos name the place only on screen or only out loud, and those we cannot read — so a link that turns up no place is not a fault. It is an ordinary result, it has a screen of its own, and it offers three ways forward. What we will never do is ask you to type the caption in. The moment the product asks a person for text they could have typed into any notes app, it has stopped being worth having.

What makes it different

Plenty of apps turn a social link into a pin on a map. No Crumbs is not trying to do more of that than anyone else. It does three things they generally do not.

  • It tells you what it is sure about. What the post actually said, what the app worked out, and what you wrote yourself stay separate. A place it is not certain about is shown to you as uncertain instead of being quietly presented as fact.
  • Finding nothing is a proper answer, not an error. Most posts do not name a place in words, so that outcome gets its own screen with real options — not a shrug.
  • What you save is genuinely yours. Only things that can be kept forever are stored, so nothing expires out of your library. And your map is private by default, enforced where the data lives.

Section 02 · the problem

Problem Statement & Business Value

Collecting works perfectly. Getting it back is broken — and getting it back is the only moment that matters.

Recommendations for real places — restaurants, cafés, bars, viewpoints, shops — increasingly arrive as social video, and the natural response is to save the post. TikTok stores that save as a video, in the order you saved it. It does not store where the place is, what kind of place it is, or what it is called. So the collecting half works, the retrieval half does not, and every question a person actually asks of a saved collection is a question about location:

  1. I'm in this neighbourhood right now — what did I save near me?
  2. I land in Tokyo on Thursday — what do I already have there?
  3. I want dinner tonight and I don't want to scroll.

A thumbnail in a saved folder rarely shows the name or the city, so answering any of these means opening videos one at a time and reading them. You do that standing up, with a decision waiting on it.

Turning a post into a place is work the platform has no reason to do, so today the person does it in their own head, from a grid of thumbnails, at exactly the wrong time.

Why this is worth building

The hard part is already done. People have already scrolled, judged, and decided that a place is worth remembering. Nothing here asks them to start a new habit — it takes a collection they already have and makes it reachable.

What comes out is worth more than what went in: a real name, real coordinates, a category, your note, and a link back to the video, held in your own account.

And what improves is the decision, not the search. A collection you cannot reach at the moment you are choosing is worth nothing, however large it is.

What survives, and what does not

The name, the category, the coordinates, your note and your Been marks all survive independently of the platform, but the link back to the original post is the exception — it works for as long as TikTok continues to serve that post.

Section 03 · who it is for

Target Audience

One kind of person, asking two questions about places they already saved — and that person is also the customer, because there is nobody else to sell to.

Everyday

“What did I save around here?”

The trigger is standing in a neighbourhood, deciding where to eat. It is answered by places near you, nearest first, offered on permission and never demanded.

Trips

“What do I already have in this city?”

The trigger is planning a trip, or landing somewhere. It is answered by the whole library on one map, anywhere in the world, with no location permission needed.

This is one profile, not two segments. The same person saves places in the city they live in and banks places for a trip they have not taken yet; designing for two audiences here would produce two half-products.

Two facts that shape every decision

The first is where this happens: on a phone, standing up, mid-scroll. Mobile is not the smaller version of this product — it is the product. Sheets you can drag, actions within thumb reach, nothing important stranded at the top of a tall screen. Desktop works, but it comes second.

The second is that adding costs and finding pays. Every tap spent adding a place is spent before the person has got anything back at all. That is why the effort goes there, and why we hold their side of it to one paste.

Who this is not for

  • People looking for somewhere new. There is no feed, no ranking, and the app never suggests a place you did not save.
  • Trip planners who want day-by-day routing, bookings or opening hours. Different product, different data.
  • Creators and businesses who want an audience. No follower graph, no public profile.
  • Anyone with only a handful of places. Below a certain size a plain list beats a map, which is why the first session has to end with several places saved.

The customer: the same person as the user, and nobody else

There is no advertiser, no business account and nobody buying access to anyone's data or attention, so the product's only obligation is to the person whose map it is. That is why privacy works the way it does: a private map, ownership enforced where the data lives, and your live location never kept on our side. No price is proposed, and nothing in this version was built to make money. If that changed, the obvious direction would be a paid tier for the heaviest savers, because they feel the problem most — but that is a direction, not a plan.

Goal 5 carries the other four. A notes app already gives you goals 1 to 4, if you are willing to type them in. Every scope decision here exists to keep the user's half of the work down to one paste.

What success looks like

Not “the search was faster”. The outcome that matters is that the saved recommendation was actually used — that the place someone kept months ago is the place they walk into tonight.

Two things follow from that. Adding happens in batches and finding happens in the moment, so each gets its own surface rather than a compromise between them.

And the first session has to end with places on the map, because an empty map is worth nothing. Onboarding here is not teaching people how the product works. It is getting their first few places in.

What we store, and why that is a business decision

Everything kept is restricted to what can be held forever — name, category, coordinates, the link to the video, and your own note. No opening hours, no photos, no ratings, no reviews; those come with expiry dates attached, and a library assembled from them is rented rather than owned. That limit is what makes goal 4 true rather than a hope.

Section 05 · what it can do

Product Capabilities

Twenty-two capabilities, all built, and they amount to six things a person can do.

01 · Get a post in without tidying anything up

Any shape of TikTok link works — a short link, a link with tracking attached, a regional variant, or the whole block of text the share button produces. A link we do not support, such as Instagram or YouTube, is recognised by name before any work starts and handed straight to adding a place by name, so a wrong link costs nothing. Pasting the same link twice gives the same answer rather than a fresh roll of the dice.

02 · Have the post read for you

No typing, no pasting text, no screenshots: the app fetches the post and reads the caption for the places it names. Finding none is a legitimate answer with a screen built for it, and given what captions are like, it is a common one.

03 · Get real places, not just names

A name is not a location, so every name found in a caption is looked up in a places directory until it has real coordinates and a real identity of its own.

When a name cannot be matched, the place is still kept. It is placed roughly, where the caption suggests it is, but it is marked as approximate, drawn differently, and never shown as confirmed. And one name that cannot be matched never costs you the other places in the same video.

04 · Decide before anything is saved

Nothing is saved until you confirm it, and the review screen says so. Every place on your map was put there by a person. Confident matches arrive already ticked and uncertain ones arrive as a short choice with nothing ticked. No score or percentage is ever shown. Each card links to that place on a public map instead, because something you can look at beats a number you have to interpret. If some places could not be saved, you are told how many.

05 · Get it back by looking

The map is the home surface, and it opens framed around your own places. Zoomed far out it shows countries; closer in, areas with a name and a count; closer still, individual pins coloured by category.

A list sits beside the map and can never disagree with it, because they are the same places. Search by name, filter by category, tap a tag, or show only the places you have Been to. Places near you are offered once, shortly after the map opens, and not again if you decline.

You can also ask for what you want in a sentence. It sets the filters and moves the map; it never adds places of its own, and one tap puts everything back.

06 · Keep it yours, and be able to leave

However many videos pointed at the same place, it is one pin. Each save keeps its own link back to the video it came from, so why did I save this? always has an answer. Mark a place Been, or not yet. Group places into collections and share one by link, as an editor or a viewer. See your library summarised — how many places, in how many cities and countries. And delete everything in one action.

How these answer the goals

Each group answers a goal. Get it in exists for goal 5 — the app does the conversion, so the user's half stays one paste. Decide and keep exists for goal 2, because a place is only structured if it has a real identity someone confirmed. Get it back is goals 1 and 3: a glance instead of a search, at the moment the decision is being made. And the restriction to what lasts forever — name, category, coordinates, note, link — is goal 4, present in every capability by what it refuses to store.

Three guarantees behind the set

  • Privacy is enforced where the data lives, not by each screen remembering to check. Your map is private because the data itself refuses to be read by anyone else.
  • A caption is words to read, never instructions to follow. The words we read belong to somebody else, and they are handled that way throughout.
  • Your notes stay yours when you share. People see the places, their categories and who added what — not your note, your tags, your Been marks, or the video you saved a place from.

What it deliberately cannot do

Four gaps, named here rather than left to be discovered.

  • It cannot read audio or on-screen text. This is the single change that would most widen what it can read. Deferred on cost and speed, not refused on principle.
  • There is no way to export your library. The most obvious thing missing from a product that says what you keep is yours.
  • A place it cannot put on a map is dropped, not held. There is nowhere to keep it for later, so a small part of what a caption offered is lost that way.
  • It cannot work offline or appear in the phone's share sheet. A web app cannot enter it.

Section 06 · how it is used

Core User Flows

These are the six core processes the product lets a person carry out, in the order they meet them. Two are drawn, because their shape is the argument.

01 · Your first places

The product is worth nothing at zero places, so the first session has one job: end with places on the map.

  1. A visitor lands on a page that says what this does in three steps — paste a TikTok link, check what we found, it lands on your map — and states the boundary right there.
  2. They create an account. It is private from the first second; there is nothing public to set up.
  3. They see an empty map that is designed rather than blank: one line, one button, and a map already looking at a sensible part of the world.

No checklist, no progress meter, no illustration, no permission prompt. The one button adds a TikTok link, and the screen it opens carries real example videos for anyone with nothing of their own to hand. The create menu on every tab also offers adding a place by name, for the person who does not need a video at all. The bar for this flow is not comprehension. It is places on the map before the person closes the tab.

02 · Add a TikTok link

The flagship, and the one flow the whole product is built around.

The drawing shows the shape. What it cannot show is the timing, which is most of the experience. The post you pasted appears almost immediately — its picture, the handle, the caption — and stays in front of you while the slower work runs underneath, so you are looking at something the whole time rather than at a spinner.

Some links never leave the browser at all: a link that is not a TikTok video is recognised on the spot and handed to adding by name, before anything is sent anywhere.

Both of the endings drawn here are normal ones. The only real failure in the product is a video the app cannot read at all, and it is rare enough to mention in a sentence rather than draw.

The review step is where the whole design lands. One card per place, confident matches already ticked, uncertain ones offered as a short choice with nothing chosen for you. You can change any of it, add a note, and untick whatever you do not want, and nothing has been written down yet. Pasting the same link again gets you the same answer, so re-trying is cheap.

The last box is the point of all the others. A pin per place, each keeping its link back to the post, waiting for a day that is usually weeks away.

03 · When a post names no place

Not an error, and the screen this product shows most often. It says which of three things happened, because they are not the same problem.

Three ways forward, strongest first. Add it by name — you watched the video, so type the name, pick it from what comes back, and you are done in fewer steps than the flow you just left. Or tell us what you remember, one sentence, revealed only when you want it and never sitting on screen as a form. Or open the original video. What never appears is a request to type the caption in.

Adding a place by name also stands on its own, from the create menu on any tab. One field takes either a link or a name, and what you typed decides what happens:

  • a TikTok link hands off to the flow above;
  • an unsupported link falls through to adding by name;
  • a name you have already saved reveals that place on your map instead of saving it twice;
  • a name you have not saved before is looked up and saved in one step — and if the directory finds nothing, nothing is saved and you are told.

Reached from the no-places screen, where you are recovering rather than sure, it behaves differently: it shows what it found and asks you to pick, and it links the save to the video that sent you there.

04 · Find it again

The payoff. It comes fourth because it happens on the second day, not because it matters least. Everything above is the price of this.

This is the one flow that is not a sequence. The three tiers in the drawing are the same library rendered at three distances, and you move between them by pinching, not by taking steps. The map opens framed around your own places, so the first thing you see is somewhere you have been thinking about rather than a default point on the globe.

Two things the drawing cannot tell you. Area pills are thinned as you zoom so their names stay readable, and pin labels are allowed to overlap rather than disappear, because a slightly crowded label beats a pin that silently vanishes. And near me is genuinely optional: it is offered once, a refusal is remembered, distances appear only when the fix is accurate enough to be worth showing, and your live location is never kept on our side.

Tap a pin and the whole record is there, including the video that made you save it. The list beside the map is the other half of this, and it shows exactly the same places, filtered the same way.

05 · Share a collection

A collection is the same map with a smaller set of pins on it. That is why sharing one did not turn this into a different product.

  1. Make a collection, name it, and put places from your library in it.
  2. Generate a link and choose whether the person joins as an editor or a viewer. One live invite per collection; making a new one plainly retires the last.
  3. Before you share, you are told plainly what you are agreeing to — anyone with the link who signs in can open this collection. One tap opens the detail, behind a control that says what it opens, and it always shows both halves together: what the other person will see, and what stays yours.
  4. They open the link. Signed out, they get a real explanation before being asked to make an account; signed in, they see who invited them and what they will be able to do.
  5. Joining asks for a name to be known by first, because everyone else in the collection will see it beside the places that person added.
  6. Both now see the collection on their own map, and a viewer can copy a shared place into their own library.

06 · Leave, without taking anything away from anyone else

Rules rather than a flow.

Delete my data removes the account and everything in it, in one action, and deleting a single place is separately available whenever you want it.

It refuses, and explains, when deleting would take something away from other people. If you own a collection other people are in, deletion stops and names those collections, with a way to go and sort it out. A refusal that tells you what to do next is not a dead end.

One thing survives, and the product says so: a note you wrote inside someone else's shared collection stays with them, with your name taken off it. No screen claims everything you wrote is gone.
