Section 01 · what this is

Product Overview

No Crumbs is a private map of the places you chose, built from links you already save.

You paste one link. The app reads the post, finds the places the caption names, gives each one real coordinates and a real identity, and shows you what it found. You confirm what you want. It lands on your map, with the video that convinced you one tap away.

The map is not a feature of this product; it is how you get things back. Location replaces search as the way a person reaches their own saved places, and everything else — what is stored, what is shown, what is refused — follows from that.

Yours

The boundary we hold, stated once

We read what a post says in words. A great many TikTok videos name the place only on screen or only out loud, and those we cannot read — so a link that yields no place is not a breakage. It is an ordinary result, it has a screen of its own, and it offers three ways forward. What we will never do is ask you to type the caption in. The moment the product asks a person for text they could have typed into any notes app, it has stopped being worth having.

Where this sits

Turning a social link into a pin on a personal map is a well-populated idea, and No Crumbs does not win by having more of it. It defends three things instead. Honesty about what the app worked out, so what the post said, what the app inferred and what you wrote stay separable, and an uncertain result is shown as uncertain. A common failure that is designed rather than patched, because finding nothing is a result and deserves its own screen. And a library that is genuinely yours, because only what can be kept forever is stored, and privacy is enforced where the data lives.

Section 02 · the problem

Problem Statement & Business Value

Collecting works perfectly. Getting it back is broken — and getting it back is the only moment that matters.

Recommendations for real places — restaurants, cafés, bars, viewpoints, shops — increasingly arrive as social video, and the natural response is to save the post. TikTok stores that save as a video, in the order you saved it. It does not store where the place is, what kind of place it is, or what it is called. So the collecting half works, the retrieval half does not, and every question a person actually asks of a saved collection is a question about location:

  1. I'm in this neighbourhood right now — what did I save near me?
  2. I land in Tokyo on Thursday — what do I already have there?
  3. I want dinner tonight and I don't want to scroll.

A thumbnail in a saved folder rarely shows the name or the city, so answering any of those means opening posts one at a time and reading them — work you do standing up, at the worst possible moment, with a decision waiting on it.

Turning a post into a place is work the platform has no reason to do, so today the person does it in their own head, from a grid of thumbnails, at exactly the wrong time.

Why this is worth building rather than tolerating

The expensive half is already paid for: people have done the scrolling, the judgement and the decision that a place is worth remembering, so this is not a new habit to learn but a collection they already have, unlocked. What comes out lasts longer than what went in — a real name, real coordinates, a category, your note and a link back, in your own account. And the improvement is to the decision, not merely to the search. A collection that cannot be reached at the moment of choosing has an effective value of zero, whatever its size.

One clause we keep honest

The name, the category, the coordinates, your note and your Been marks all survive independently of the platform, but the link back to the original post is the exception — it works for as long as TikTok continues to serve that post.

Section 03 · who it is for

Target Audience

One person with two retrieval questions, who is also the customer — nobody else is buying access to them.

Everyday

“What did I save around here?”

The trigger is standing in a neighbourhood, deciding where to eat. It is answered by places near you, nearest first, offered on permission and never demanded.

Trips

“What do I already have in this city?”

The trigger is planning a trip, or landing somewhere. It is answered by the whole library on one map, anywhere in the world, with no location permission needed.

This is one profile, not two segments. The same person saves places in the city they live in and banks places for a trip they have not taken yet; designing for two audiences here would produce two half-products.

Two facts that shape every decision

This happens on a phone, standing up, mid-scroll, so mobile is not the smaller version of this product — it is the product: sheets you can drag, actions inside thumb reach, nothing important stranded at the top of a tall screen, and desktop supported but secondary. And adding is the cost while finding is the reward. Every tap it takes to add a place is paid before any value arrives, which is why the quality budget is spent there and why the person's side of the exchange is held to one paste.

Who this is not for

Stated plainly, so the boundary can be argued with.

  • People looking for somewhere new. There is no feed, no ranking, and the app never suggests a place you did not save.
  • Trip planners who want day-by-day routing, bookings or opening hours. Different product, different data.
  • Creators and businesses who want an audience. No follower graph, no public profile.
  • Anyone with a handful of places. Below a certain size a plain list beats a map — which makes the first session a real constraint, not a footnote.

The user and the customer are the same person

There is no advertiser, no business account and nobody buying access to anyone's data or attention, so the product's only obligation is to the person whose map it is. That is why privacy works the way it does: a private map, ownership enforced where the data lives, and your live location never kept on our side. No price is proposed and nothing in this version was built to make money. If it ever were, the natural direction is a paid tier for the heaviest savers, because they feel the problem most — but that is a direction, not a plan.

Goal 5 carries the other four. Goals 1 to 4 describe things a notes app already gives you if you are willing to type; every scope decision in this product exists to keep the user's side of the exchange down to one paste.

What success looks like

Not “the search was faster”. The outcome that matters is that the saved recommendation was actually used — that the place someone kept months ago is the place they walk into tonight. Two things follow. Adding is a batch activity and finding is an in-the-moment one, so each gets its own surface instead of compromising to share one. And the first session has to end with places on the map, because the product is worthless empty: onboarding is not education, it is getting the first few places in.

Everything kept is restricted to what can be held forever — name, category, coordinates, the link to the post, and your own note. No opening hours, no photos, no ratings, no reviews; those come with expiry dates attached, and a library assembled from them is rented rather than owned. That restriction is the reason goal 4 can be said out loud.

Section 05 · what it can do

Product Capabilities

Twenty-two capabilities, all built, and they amount to six things a person can do.

Get a post in without tidying anything up

Any shape of TikTok link works — a short link, a link with tracking attached, a regional variant, or the whole block of text the share button produces. A link we do not support, such as Instagram or YouTube, is recognised by name before any work starts and handed straight to adding a place by name, so a wrong link costs nothing. Pasting the same link twice gives the same answer rather than a fresh roll of the dice.

Have the post read for you

No typing, no pasting text, no screenshots: the app fetches the post and reads the caption for the places it names. Finding none is a legitimate answer with a screen built for it, and given what captions are like, it is a common one.

Get real places, not just names

A name is not a location, so every candidate is matched against a places directory until it has real coordinates and a real identity. When a name cannot be matched, the place is still kept — using the position the caption implies — but it is marked as approximate, drawn differently, and never presented as confirmed. One name that cannot be matched never costs you the rest of the post.

Decide before anything is saved

Nothing is written until you confirm; the review screen says so and every place on your map was put there by a person. Confident matches arrive already ticked and uncertain ones arrive as a short choice with nothing ticked. No score or percentage is ever shown — each card links out to a public map instead, because something you can check beats something you have to interpret. If some places could not be saved you are told how many.

Get it back by looking

The map is the home surface and opens framed around your own places. Zoomed far out it shows countries, then areas with a name and a count, then individual pins coloured by category. A list sits beside it and can never disagree with the pins, because they are the same places: search by text, filter by category, tap a tag, or show only what you have Been to. Places near you are offered shortly after opening and only once. And you can ask for what you want in a sentence — it sets filters and moves the map, never returns places of its own, and one tap undoes it.

Keep it yours, and be able to leave

However many videos pointed at the same place, it is one pin, and every save keeps its own link back to the post that recommended it, so why did I save this? is always answerable. Mark a place Been, or not yet. Group places into collections and share one by link, as an editor or a viewer. See your library summarised — how many places, in how many cities and countries. And delete everything in one action.

Three guarantees behind the set

Privacy is enforced where the data lives, not by each screen remembering to check — your map is private because the data itself refuses to be read by anyone else. A caption is treated as words to read and never as instructions to follow; the content we ingest is somebody else's, and it is handled that way throughout. And your notes stay yours when you share: people see the places, their categories and who added what, not your note, your tags, your Been marks or the video you saved it from.

What it deliberately cannot do

Four gaps, named rather than left to be discovered. It cannot read audio or on-screen text, which is the single change that would most widen what it can read — deferred on cost and speed, not refused on principle. There is no way to export your library, which is the most obvious thing missing from a product that says what you keep is yours. A place it cannot map at all is not parked anywhere, so a small amount of what a caption offered is lost. And it cannot work offline or appear in the phone's share sheet, because a web app cannot enter it.

Section 06 · how it is used

Core User Flows

Six flows, in the order a person meets them; two are drawn, because their shape is the argument.

01 · Your first places

The product is worth nothing at zero places, so the first session has one job: end with places on the map.

  1. A visitor lands on a page that says what this does in three steps — paste a TikTok link, check what we found, it lands on your map — and states the boundary right there.
  2. They create an account. It is private from the first second; there is nothing public to set up.
  3. They see an empty map that is designed rather than blank: one line, one button, and a map already looking at a sensible part of the world.

No checklist, no progress meter, no illustration, no permission prompt. The one button adds a TikTok link, and the screen it opens carries real example videos for someone who has nothing of their own to hand; the create menu on any tab also offers adding a place by name, for the person who does not need a post at all. The bar for this flow is not comprehension. It is places on the map before the person closes the tab.

02 · Add a TikTok link

The flagship: eight stages, four places it can legitimately diverge, and one genuine hard stop.

The drawing shows the shape. What it cannot show is the timing, which is most of the experience. The post you pasted appears almost immediately — its picture, the handle, the caption — and stays in front of you while the slower work runs underneath, so you are looking at something the whole time rather than at a spinner.

Two of the diverges cost nothing at all, because they are settled in the browser before a single request goes out: a link that is not a TikTok video is recognised on the spot and handed to adding by name. The gold outlines are the other normal endings, and there are three of them. Only the dashed red box is a real failure — the post itself could not be read — and it is the only one in the product.

The review step is where the whole design lands. One card per place, confident matches already ticked, uncertain ones offered as a short choice with nothing chosen for you. You can change any of it, add a note, and untick whatever you do not want, and nothing above that dashed line has been written down. Pasting the same link again gets you the same answer, so re-trying is cheap.

The last node is the point of the other seven. A pin per place, each keeping its link back to the post, waiting for a day that is usually weeks away.

03 · When a post names no place

Not an error, and the screen this product shows most often. It says which of three things happened, because they are not the same problem.

Two ways forward are offered, strongest first. Add it by name — you watched the video, so type the name, pick it from what comes back, and you are done in fewer steps than the flow you just left. Or tell us what you remember, one sentence, revealed only when you want it, never on screen as a form. Opening the original post is always available as a third. What never appears is a request to type the caption in.

Adding a place by name also stands on its own, from the create menu on any tab. One field takes either a link or a name, and what you typed decides what happens: a TikTok link hands off to the import, an unsupported link falls through to adding by name, text matching something you already saved reveals that place instead of duplicating it, and text with no match saves the top result in one step. Reached from the no-places screen, where you are recovering rather than sure, it shows you what it found and asks you to pick — and links the save to the post that prompted it.

04 · Find it again

The payoff. Listed fourth because it happens on the second day, not because it matters least — everything above is the price of this.

This is the one flow that is not a sequence. The three tiers in the drawing are the same library rendered at three distances, and you move between them by pinching, not by taking steps. The map opens framed around your own places, so the first thing you see is somewhere you have been thinking about rather than a default point on the globe.

Two things the drawing cannot tell you. Area pills are thinned as you zoom so their names stay readable, and pin labels are allowed to overlap rather than disappear — a label you can read at an angle beats a pin that silently drops out. And near me is genuinely optional: it is offered once, a refusal is remembered, distances appear only when the fix is accurate enough to be worth showing, and your live location is never kept on our side.

The list beside the map is the other half of this, and can never disagree with the pins because they are the same places. Search a name, filter by category, tap a tag, hide what you have Been to, or type what you want as a sentence and let it set the filters. Tap a pin and the whole record is there, including the video that made you save it.

05 · Share a collection

A collection is your map with a different set of pins — which is why it did not widen what this product is.

  1. Make a collection, name it, and put places from your library in it.
  2. Generate a link and choose whether the person joins as an editor or a viewer. One live invite per collection; making a new one plainly retires the last.
  3. Before you share, the access fact is stated outright — anyone with the link who signs in can open this collection. Which fields they see sits one tap away, under a control that names what is behind it, and the two halves are always shown together.
  4. They open the link. Signed out, they get a real explanation before being asked to make an account; signed in, they see who invited them and what they will be able to do.
  5. Joining asks what to call them first, because it makes them visible to others as who added what.
  6. Both now see the collection on their own map, and a viewer can copy a shared place into their own library.

06 · Leave, and take nothing with you by accident

Rules rather than a flow.

Delete my data removes the account and everything in it, in one action, and deleting a single place is separately available whenever you want it.

It refuses, and explains, when deleting would take something away from other people. If you own a collection other people are in, deletion stops and names those collections, with a way to go and sort it out. A refusal that tells you what to do is a different thing from a refusal.

One thing survives, and the product says so: a note you wrote inside someone else's shared collection stays with them, with your name taken off it. No screen claims everything you wrote is gone.
