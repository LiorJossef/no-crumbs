Section 01 · what this is

Product Overview

No Crumbs is a private map of the places you chose, built from links you already save.

You paste one link. The app reads the post, finds the places the caption names, gives each one real coordinates and a real identity, and shows you what it found. You confirm, and it lands on your map with the video that convinced you one tap away.

The map is not a feature; it is how you get things back — and what we store, what we show and what we refuse all follow from that. Three things make it unusual: what the post said, what the app worked out and what you wrote stay separate, so an uncertain place is shown as uncertain; finding nothing is a proper answer with a screen of its own; and only what can be kept forever is stored, on a map that is private by default and enforced where the data lives.

1.1  What we refuse

One link, TikTok only, and only the caption. Its oEmbed endpoint is the one official way to read a post’s caption without asking the person to paste it in, so words are all we get — and many TikToks name the place only on screen or out loud. A link that turns up no place is therefore the ordinary result, not a fault: it has a screen of its own and three ways forward. What we will never do is ask you to type the caption in.

The measured number. Of eleven real recommendation TikToks, three named a place we could resolve: roughly 27%, about one link in four. That is a small sample with a wide interval. It is also why “no places found” is a designed surface rather than an error path — at that rate it is the outcome a person meets most often.

Section 02 · the problem

Problem Statement & Business Value

Collecting works perfectly. Getting it back is broken — and getting it back is the only moment that matters.

Recommendations for real places — restaurants, cafés, bars, viewpoints, shops — arrive as social video now, and the natural response is to save the post. TikTok stores that save as a video, in the order you saved it: not where the place is, not what kind of place it is, not what it is called. So the collecting half works and the retrieval half does not — and every question a person actually asks of a saved collection is a question about location:

  1. I’m in this neighbourhood right now — what did I save near me?
  2. I land in Tokyo on Thursday — what do I already have there?
  3. I want dinner tonight and I don’t want to scroll.

A thumbnail rarely shows the name or the city, and the caption under it usually names no place either. Answering any of these means opening videos one at a time, standing up, with a decision waiting on it — work the platform has no reason to do, so the person does it in their own head at exactly the wrong moment.

2.2  Why this is worth building

The hard part is already done. People have scrolled, judged and decided that a place is worth remembering; nothing here asks for a new habit. What comes out is worth more than what went in: a name, a category, coordinates, tags, your note and the link back to the post, held in your own account.

And what improves is the decision, not the search. A collection you cannot reach while you are choosing is worth nothing, however large.

What survives, and what does not

The name, category, coordinates, tags, note and Been marks survive independently of the platform. The link back to the original post is the exception — it works for as long as TikTok serves that post.

Section 03 · who it is for

Target Audience

One kind of person, asking two questions about places they already saved — and that person is also the customer, because there is nobody else to sell to.

Everyday

“What did I save around here?”

Standing in a neighbourhood, deciding where to eat. Answered by places near you, nearest first, offered on permission and never demanded.

Trips

“What do I already have in this city?”

Planning a trip, or landing somewhere. Answered by the whole library on one map, anywhere in the world, with no location permission needed.

This is one profile, not two segments: the same person saves at home and banks places for a trip not yet taken. Who it is not for is 1.1.

3.1  Two facts that shape every decision

This happens on a phone, standing up, mid-scroll. Mobile is not the smaller version of this product — it is the product: draggable sheets, actions within thumb reach, nothing important stranded at the top of a tall screen. Desktop comes second. And adding costs while finding pays — every tap spent adding is spent before the person has got anything back — which is why their half is one paste.

3.2  The customer: the same person as the user, and nobody else

There is no advertiser and nobody buying anyone’s data or attention, so the product’s only obligation is to the person whose map it is. That is why privacy works as it does: a private map, ownership enforced where the data lives, and your live location never kept on our side. No price is proposed; if one were, a paid tier for the heaviest savers is the obvious direction — a direction, not a plan.

Section 04 · what we are trying to achieve

Business Goals

Three goals, each with the thing that would show it had been met — and the third is the one that carries the other two.

01

Loose social saves become structured places you own

A name, a category, coordinates, tags, your note and the link back to the post — held by location, in your own account.

How you would know: all six read off a saved place without opening TikTok, each marked as what the post said, what the app worked out or what you wrote; and nothing stored expires, so a library left alone for a year still reads correctly.

02

Getting a place back takes a glance, and the recommendation changes the decision

Answered by looking at a map, not by working through a folder, so the recommendation is in front of you while you choose instead of forgotten at it.

How you would know: a person reaches the place they wanted without typing anything, and a place saved weeks earlier gets marked Been.

03

The conversion costs the user nothing

The app turns the video into a place; you supply a link.

How you would know: the user’s half of an import is one paste and one confirm, and no screen asks them to type in what the post said.

Goal 3 carries the other two. A notes app gives you goals 1 and 2 if you are willing to type them in. Every scope decision here keeps the user’s half down to one paste.

4.1  What success looks like, and what we store

Not “the search was faster”, but that the saved recommendation was actually used — the place someone kept months ago is the place they walk into tonight. And the first session must end with places on the map, because an empty map is worth nothing: onboarding here is not teaching the product, it is getting the first few places in.

A place record holds six things and no more: name, category, coordinates, your tags, your note, the link back to the post. No opening hours, photos, ratings or reviews — those expire, and a library assembled from them is rented rather than owned. That limit is what makes the durability in goal 1 true rather than a hope.

Section 05 · what it can do

Product Capabilities

Every capability below is built, in three groups: getting a post in, deciding what to keep, and getting it back. Section 06 shows each of them in use.

Get it in

Any shape of TikTok link works — short links, tracking parameters, regional variants, or the whole block of text the share button produces. An Instagram or YouTube link is recognised before any work starts and handed to adding a place by name. The app reads the post itself: no typing, no pasted text, no screenshots.

Decide and keep

Nothing is saved until you confirm it, so every place on your map was put there by a person. Confident matches arrive ticked, uncertain ones as a short choice with nothing ticked; no score is shown, and each card links to that place on a public map instead. However many videos point at one place it is one pin, each save keeping its own link back to the video.

Get it back

The map is the home surface and opens framed around your own places: countries far out, named areas with a count closer in, category-coloured pins closer still. A list beside it holds the same places — search, filter by category, tap a tag, or show only where you have Been. Also built: places near you offered once, asking in a sentence, collections shared by link as editor or viewer, a library summary, and delete everything in one action.

5.1  How a link becomes a pin

Whatever shape of link you paste reduces to one canonical post address, and TikTok’s oEmbed endpoint returns the caption. A language model turns that caption into structured places, with the category checked against a fixed list so it cannot invent a kind of place, and what it inferred kept separate from what the caption said. Each name then goes to Google’s places directory for coordinates and an identity; a name that matches nothing is still kept, placed roughly and marked approximate. Nothing is written until you confirm.

The stack — Next.js on Vercel, Supabase Postgres with row-level security, MapLibre on CARTO — and the pipeline drawn step by step are the Technical Design document, section 03; the same path from the user’s side is 6.2. Searching in a sentence uses a small model too, but it may only choose filters your library already has, so it can never invent a place.

5.2  Three guarantees behind the set

  • Privacy is enforced where the data lives, not by each screen remembering to check. One kind of account — the person whose map it is — plus a signed-out visitor who sees only the landing and sign-in pages, and a collection member who sees that one collection.
  • A caption is words to read, never instructions to follow. The words belong to somebody else, and are handled that way throughout.
  • Your notes stay yours when you share. The two halves of that, line by line, are the table in 6.5.

5.3  Not yet

Four gaps, in the order we would close them.

  1. No way to export your library. The obvious omission from a product that says what you keep is yours, and the cheapest to fix.
  2. A place we cannot match is placed roughly. Marked approximate and honest about it, but it should be upgraded to a real identity when one can be found.
  3. It cannot read audio or on-screen text. The single change that would most widen what it reads, since most videos name the place only out loud or on screen. Deferred on cost and speed, not refused on principle.
  4. No offline support, and the one-tap share route works on Android and needs a shortcut on iPhone.

Section 06 · how it is used

Core User Flows

The six things a person actually does, in the order they meet them. Two are drawn, because their shape is the argument.

6.1 · Your first places

The product is worth nothing at zero places, so the first session has one job: end with places on the map.

  1. A visitor lands on a page that says what this does in three steps — paste a TikTok link, check what we found, it lands on your map — and states the boundary there.
  2. They create an account, private from the first second, with nothing public to set up.
  3. They see an empty map that is designed rather than blank: one line, one button, and a map already looking at a sensible part of the world.

No checklist, no progress meter, no permission prompt. The button adds a TikTok link, and that screen carries real example videos for anyone with nothing of their own to hand. The bar is not comprehension; it is places on the map before the person closes the tab.

6.2 · Add a TikTok link

The flagship, and the one flow the whole product is built around.

What the drawing cannot show is the timing, and that is most of the experience. The post appears almost immediately — picture, handle, caption — and stays in front of you while the slower work runs underneath, so you never watch a spinner. Both endings are normal and the right-hand one is more common; the only real failure is a video the app cannot read at all, which is rare.

The review step is where the design lands. One card per place, confident matches already ticked, uncertain ones a short choice with nothing chosen for you. Change any of it, add a note, untick what you do not want: nothing is written down until you press save. Then a pin per place, each keeping its link back to the post, waiting for a day that is usually weeks away.

6.3 · When a post names no place

Not an error, and the screen this product shows most often. It says what happened — no caption at all, or a caption that named nothing we could map, or one that named a city but no place — and then offers three ways on, strongest first.

  • Add it by name. You watched the video, so type the name and pick it from what comes back. The save still remembers the video that sent you there.
  • Tell us what you remember. One sentence, shown only if you want it.
  • Open the original video. Always available.

Adding by name also stands on its own, from the create menu on any tab. One field takes a link or a name: a TikTok link starts the flow above, a new name is looked up and saved in one step, and a name you already have is revealed on your map rather than saved twice.

6.4 · Find it again

The payoff. It comes fourth because it happens on the second day, not because it matters least — everything above is the price of this.

This is the one flow that is not a sequence: the three tiers are the same library drawn at three distances, and you move between them by pinching. The map opens framed around your own places, so the first thing you see is somewhere you have been thinking about rather than a default point on the globe.

Near me is genuinely optional — offered once, a refusal remembered, distances shown only when the fix is accurate enough to be worth it, and your live location never kept on our side. Tap a pin and the whole record is there, including the video that made you save it; the list beside the map shows the same places, filtered the same way.

6.5 · Share a collection

A collection is the same map with fewer pins on it, which is why sharing one did not turn this into a different product.

  1. Make a collection, name it, and put places from your library in it.
  2. Generate a link and choose whether the person joins as editor or viewer. One live invite per collection; a new one retires the last.
  3. Before you share, you are told what you are agreeing to: anyone with the link who signs in can open this collection. The detail is one tap away and shows both halves.
  4. They sign in and choose a name to be known by, since everyone in the collection sees it beside the places that person added. Both now see the collection on their own map, and a viewer can copy a shared place into their own library.

6.6 · Leave, without taking anything away from anyone else

Rules rather than a flow.

Delete my data removes the account and everything in it in one action, and a single place can be deleted whenever you want. It refuses, and explains, when deleting would take something away from other people: if you own a collection other people are in, deletion stops, names those collections and gives you a way to sort it out. And one thing survives, which the product says out loud — a note you wrote inside someone else’s shared collection stays with them, with your name taken off it.
