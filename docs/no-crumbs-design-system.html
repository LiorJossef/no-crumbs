Which means these words are gone

Every one of these roots is already carrying someone else's product in this exact category. A name built from them is not distinctive, it is a footnote in someone else's App Store search results.

The strategy this forces, and it happens to be the right one anyway

The reference tier you named — Linear, Notion, Hex, Asana — shares one naming move: a real word used obliquely. None of them describes its mechanism. A line, an idea, a shape, a yoga pose. They are ownable precisely because the word arrives from an adjacent world and the product gets to define what it means there. That is now not just the most distinctive route available here — after the seventeen above, it is the only one left.

The test

A name here is judged on the failure screen, not the logo.

This is the rule the product lead set during the audit and it is the only one that reliably kills a pretty name. At the measured extraction hit rate, "no places named" is the modal outcome of an import — roughly three imports in four. So the most-read sentence in the entire product is not the success line. It is the apology that must not sound like one.

1. The header

The bare word, alone, in the shell. It has to look like a product at 13 px uppercase and at 40 px on the sign-in hero, and it has to be findable in a browser tab at 16 px.

2. The success line

The confirm screen. "3 places ___". Any name that needs a preposition and four extra words here is paying rent on every import.

3. The failure line — the discriminator

"Nothing to ___ from this post." Matter-of-fact, blameless, offering a next move. This is the string that disqualified Landed, which I recommended before the audit: pins land, but nothing "un-lands", so the most-seen screen in the product has no sentence.

The tone rules the brand document already binds us to: state, don't perform — "3 places found", never "Great news!". Concrete over technical. And the product never congratulates itself on the find, because the discovery was the user's. A name that can only be shouted fails all three.

The mark

A mascot that degrades into a map pin.

You pointed me at Zoran Milic's work — soft rounded geometry, clean medium-weight outlines, bright limited palettes, charm over realism. So this is a character, not an abstract mark. The thing that makes it a system rather than a drawing is that the same silhouette works at every size: it is a mascot at 168 px, a pin at 30 px, and a legible blob at 16 px. One shape, four jobs.

Why the pin is the whole idea

All thirty-four products in this category draw the same pin: a perfect teardrop with a circular head. It is the Google Maps marker and it belongs to nobody. A crumb-shaped pin is instantly ours — irregular, soft-cornered, recognisable at 15 px, and it still takes the category colour so it keeps doing its job as information. The mascot is not decoration bolted onto the product; it is the pin, wearing a face on the surfaces that can afford one.

Where the face goes, and where it doesn't

The face appears on brand surfaces — app icon, splash, sign-in, empty states, the link preview. It never appears on the map. Thirty-one smiling faces scattered over Tel Aviv is a toy, not a tool, and the moment a pin has eyes it stops being able to carry a category colour without looking ill. Face on the chrome, silhouette on the data.

This overrules a written decision

Flagging rather than quietly overwriting. brand-and-product-foundation.md scopes the MVP as “warm minimal” and explicitly excludes an illustration style; Charter §4 keeps illustration systems off the current sprint. A mascot is exactly that. You are the product owner and this is your call to make — I just want it recorded as a ruling, not discovered later as a contradiction.

What I can and can't draw

This is a constructed vector character — correct geometry, correct weights, correct system behaviour. It is not charm. Milic's work is charming because of proportion decisions a specialist makes by eye. Treat this as a tight brief to hand an illustrator: the silhouette, the palette, the sizes it must survive, and the rule that it has to work with the face removed.

The one thing to keep if you redraw it

The silhouette. Everything in this system — pin, favicon, trail, icon — is the same closed path at different sizes. An illustrator can change the face, the shading, the feet, the palette. If they change the outline, you lose the pin and you are back to a teardrop like everyone else.

Wordmark

Two words, stacked, heavy, tight.

Set in Fraunces at 900 — a variable display serif with a SOFT axis, run warm at 60, and WONK on, which is what gives it the slight tilt that keeps it from reading as a bank. Stacked is the primary: it makes a solid rectangle that sits beside the mascot without either fighting the other, and it puts No and Crumbs on separate lines so the phrase reads as a phrase. Fraunces carries the editorial voice and nothing else — the wordmark and the large headings. Functional labels stay in Manrope, and a wordmark is a drawn asset, not a typeface in the product — so the signed-off “Manrope only” decision for UI survives intact.

Colour

The crumb is a shape, not a colour.

This is the load-bearing decision in the whole identity, and it is a refusal. The obvious move is to make toast-gold the brand colour. I am not doing that, because gold sits a few degrees from the café category amber, and the entire point of the facelift's colour work is that on this map colour means what a place is. A brand colour competing with that would undo it. So mint stays the only brand colour, gold belongs to the mascot alone, and the two never appear on the same surface.

The most important face is the neutral one

Nothing found is flat eyes and a flat mouth — not a frown, not a shrug, not a sad droop. This is the screen a user hits on roughly three imports in four, and the tone rule is that it must be matter-of-fact and never apologetic. A sad mascot turns the product's most common outcome into a small failure eight times a week. Neutral says that happens, and moves on.

What is deliberately missing

No angry face, no crying face, no confused face, no celebration beyond a single spark pair. The product has no state that warrants them, and the moment the set has a face with no screen behind it, someone will find a screen to put it on.

Rules

Six lines that keep a mascot from eating a product.

1 · Face on chrome, silhouette on data

The character appears on the shell. On the map it is a pin in a category colour, with no face. Thirty-one smiling faces over Tel Aviv is a toy.

2 · One mascot per screen

Never two, never a crowd, never a mascot beside a wordmark that also contains it.

3 · It never speaks

No speech bubbles, no first person, no name for the character. The product's voice is the product's, and a talking mascot would break the tone rule that bans performing.

4 · It never reacts to a place

No approving face beside a restaurant, no rating, no favourite. The discovery is the user's and we do not editorialise about it.

5 · Gold stays off the map

The mascot's palette never appears where category colour lives, or colour stops meaning what a place is.

6 · No new faces without a screen

Adding a mood means naming the state it serves. If there is no state, there is no face.

Chapter 04

The Voice

The name is the only joke. Every string after it states a fact and stops.

Tone Jargon Mechanics Drift Checklist

Voice

The joke is in the name. The copy stays straight.

This is the rule that decides whether the name ages well or becomes exhausting. A funny name buys you exactly one laugh, at install. Spend it there, then write like a product that has nothing to prove — which is what the brand document already asks for: state, don't perform.

Rule 1

The name is the only joke in the product. Every string after it states a fact and stops. Not a style preference — the copy spec already bans idioms and wordplay in every string, for translation. The name is exempt because it is a proper noun. Nothing else is.

Rule 2

Never explain the pun. No “because we leave no crumbs behind!” anywhere. If a line needs the joke explained, it is the wrong line.

Rule 3

Add breadcrumb to the banned-vocabulary list on its own merits — it is navigation-component vocabulary, which the copy rules already forbid.

The contradiction in the name, and how it resolves

Worth stating plainly because someone will notice it: no crumbs means none are left, but the trail idea needs crumbs to exist. Both readings survive only if they live on different layers. The negation is the copy; the trail is the mark. The words always mean "that place was flawless" — a judgement about somewhere you went. The trail only ever appears as a shape: the loading animation, the empty state, the icon. They never meet in a sentence, so they never argue.

Rejected: my own signature line

I proposed “No crumbs in this one.” for the no-places screen and called it the signature line. The product lead struck it, and was right on all four counts. It performs — it substitutes the brand for the news, when the user needs to know what happened. The pun half-works, which is worse than not working: on a product full of restaurants, “no crumbs” reads for a beat as no food places, and a user disambiguating a joke on three imports in four stops finding it funny around the third. It breaks the copy spec's own ban on wordplay, which exists for translation. And it costs the concrete disclosure that teaches people what the product can and cannot do.

Where the name may appear — the complete list

Permitted

The shell header wordmark. The landing and sign-in mark. The browser tab title. The meta description. The app icon. The deck. That is the entire list.

Banned

Every failure and error string. Every toast — this is where brands break first. Empty and first-run states. Place detail and the review screen, or anything describing a place: a crumb-flavoured compliment about a restaurant is us editorialising about the user's own discovery. Collection default names and invite copy. Nav labels, button labels, category labels. Never a verb.

Tagline: none, in the product

There is no surface for one. The landing page was deliberately pruned to a minimal sign-in and its subhead already does that job; a tagline under the wordmark would be a second competing sentence on the only screen that has one. For the deck, where a line is compulsory: “Organised by where, not when.” — the ratified positioning's own six words, already the strongest line the product owns. Held in reserve if a lockup ever demands one: Nothing gets lost.

The real cost, and it isn't dating

“Ate and left no crumbs” peaked around 2023–24. It is not fresh, it is familiar — which cuts both ways: it will not spike and it will not fall off a cliff, because it already had one. It ages like slay, not like rizz. And what dates is copy leaning on the slang reading, which the rules above already ban.

The cost to price instead is category legibility. A negation that reads food-adjacent makes a first-time hearer guess a recipe app or a cleaning product before a map. Every introduction pays that. It is why the landing subhead must stay literal and must never be traded for a cleverer line — accept the name, accept that constraint with it.

The verdict

This is a disciplined product that never took a risk — and it currently opens on an empty map.

Six specialists read this codebase in parallel: the design system, the map shell, the import flow, the secondary surfaces, the map's real capability ceiling, and the demo itself. The direction

Three principles, and one of them is the whole pitch.

A facelift with no thesis becomes a pile of tweaks. This one has a single argument — the map should behave like it knows what is on it — and everything else is downstream of it.

Colour is information

Not decoration, not brand expression. A colour appears when it tells you something you would otherwise have to read: what a place is, whether you have been, which collection it belongs to. Mint keeps one job — what you can press — and stops trying to be the whole palette.

The map reacts

Today the map renders places. It should respond to them: a pin that grows when it is yours to look at, a filter that fades rather than deletes, a camera that moves because something happened. Aliveness is not animation volume; it is the product acknowledging every single thing you do, within one frame.

Show the source

A saved place is a memory of a video someone made. The thumbnail, the handle and the caption are already fetched and stored, and they are the difference between a database row and something you are glad you kept. Put them on screen.

Forced into scope by the facelift

Not features sneaking in — things a redesigned screen exposes and cannot ship without. The name, because a shell header needs a word. The dark tokens, because they are stale and a theme toggle would ship an abandoned palette. The import decomposition, because you cannot safely redesign 2,482 lines in one file. The zero-places first run, because a beautiful map with nothing on it is the first thing a new user sees.

Explicitly out — so nobody re-proposes them

The streaming import route (L0-F6) is a real feature and stays on the plan of record, not in here — and the good news from the audit is that the demo does not need it. Also out: the account menu and delete-my-data (L1-F8-T1, a product feature), more categories, a marketing site, onboarding, notifications, import history, and anything with a social graph. Charter §4 keeps these on the Future list.

Doing it in Tailwind, properly

The reason the interaction layer is expensive today is that the tokens are not registered as theme keys, so no utility exists for them and every call site writes an arbitrary value instead. There are 163 arbitrary-value classes in src/ and 36 registered @theme keys, all of them colours, radii and fonts. Register the rest and the utilities appear.

1 · Register, and stop writing brackets

Add shadows, easings, durations and the type scale to @theme in globals.css. Tailwind v4 generates a utility per key, so shadow-[var(--shadow-elevated)] — written at eleven call sites — becomes shadow-sheet, and duration-[220ms] becomes duration-base. The 163 arbitrary values collapse into named utilities that a reviewer can check at a glance.

2 · State in variants, not in ternaries

Selected states are currently assembled by concatenating class strings in JS. Use the variants instead: aria-pressed:bg-tag-on, data-[state=open]:rotate-180, aria-[current=true]:text-mint. The DOM already carries the attributes — there are 21 data-[ uses and exactly one aria-[, so the pattern exists and is barely used.

3 · group is how the row talks to the pin

Zero uses of group-hover: in the codebase today, which is precisely why nothing on any screen responds to a hover somewhere else. Mark the row group, then the disc, the name and the chevron all react together — and the same mechanism carries the row↔pin coupling.

4 · motion-safe:, not a global media query

Eight motion-reduce: uses and zero motion-safe:. Inverting it makes the accessible path the default: write the movement under motion-safe: and a component author cannot forget the reduced case, because the un-prefixed state is the reduced case.

5 · Press feedback is one utility

Four active: uses in the whole product. Adding active:scale-[.985] to the shared button, row and chip classes is close to a one-line change per component and is the single most noticeable interaction fix available.

6 · Entry without a library

Tailwind v4 ships a starting variant over @starting-style, so an enter animation is starting:opacity-0 starting:translate-y-1 with a transition — no JS, no AnimatePresence. Worth confirming against the installed 4.3.3 before it is relied on; keep motion for the sheet and the choreographed landing, where a spring is genuinely needed.

The order this happens in

Registration first, in stage 1 — it is a single file and it unblocks everything else. Then press feedback, which is one utility on three shared class strings. Then group coupling, which is what makes the library and the map feel like one surface rather than two. The choreographed moments — landing, band-crossing, the count tick — come last, in the stages that own those screens, because they are the only ones that need judgement rather than a utility.

Reality check

What the map can actually do — including the thing I first said it couldn't.

This project labels every third-party claim VERIFIED, ASSUMED or UNAVAILABLE, and only lets design depend on VERIFIED. A facelift plan is exactly where that rule earns its keep, because the tempting wow effects are the ones the stack may not support.

Correction — the first version of this page was wrong about 3D buildings

I inspected CARTO's style JSON, saw that Voyager draws buildings as a flat fill plus a 2 px translated fake top, found no height attribute referenced in its 93 layers, and concluded the data wasn't there. That was the wrong file. The tile schema is the authority, and carto.streets/v1/tiles.json — which I have now fetched and read myself — declares the building layer with the fields render_height, render_min_height and hide_3d. Extruded buildings are available, on tiles this product already pays nothing for. The specialist caught it; I verified it independently rather than taking the correction on trust.

The performance budget, measured

2,000 pins with labels gated: 19.0 ms median, 60.5 ms p95. The same 2,000 with labels forced on: 34.0 ms, about 29 fps. Measured on SwiftShader rather than a phone, so treat it as an upper bound and a ranking. It is why the plan tiers labels by zoom rather than turning them on.

Where the pin ceiling actually is

The product uses zero DOM markers — every pin is a canvas-rasterised bitmap in a symbol layer. That is why 2,000 pins are viable at all; DOM markers jank on a mid-range phone somewhere around 100. The constraint on putting the TikTok still inside a pin is the icon atlas, which is a texture: imagery for the selected place and the current import, category colour for everything else.

Five stages. Every one shippable, demoable, and worth showing on its own.

Foundation first, and that is not ceremony: stage 1 is what makes stages 2–5 token edits instead of rewrites, and what stops five parallel branches fighting over the same files. What could go wrong, in one sentence

The most likely way this facelift makes the product worse is that it teaches it to over-claim. Every instinct a facelift brings — richer motion, confident copy, a smoother rail, a celebratory confirm — pushes against the one rule this product is built on, that an uncertain result beats a confidently wrong one. The failure mode is not an ugly product; it is a beautiful one that fakes streamed stages and makes the most common outcome feel like a bug. So the ruling is: no change in this plan may increase what the product asserts — and that is checkable, independently, by someone who did not build it.

Owed to you

Five decisions I should not make on your behalf.

Everything else in this plan is an ordinary implementation call and I will just make it. These five are yours: one blocks a stage, one is a ruling about honesty that governs the whole demo, and one is not an engineering task at all.

Before any of this can land: GitHub Actions cannot start a runner

Twenty-two consecutive CI runs have failed in about two seconds having executed zero steps, since 2026‑08‑29 21:52. That is not a code failure — ci.yml is present, active and correct — it is an account-level Actions problem, most plausibly an exhausted minutes allowance on a private repo. Because merge:pr correctly refuses a PR whose checks are absent or failing, nothing can land until it is cleared. One page to check: github.com/settings/billing.

What we do in the meantime, and it matters more than it sounds. Stage 1 touches the token files every later stage edits, so five branches all cut from main would converge into one enormous conflicted diff the day the runner returns. We land the stages as a stacked chain — each branch based on the one before, not on main — and we hold the stack at two deep. If stage 3 would be the third unlanded branch, we stop building and fix the runner. Local npm run verify plus a real-device pass is the evidence until then, and it is not green: verify covers one of CI's four jobs.

1. The name — blocks stage 2

Owed at L1-F1-T1, first string in the shell header, and a placeholder in every mockup on this page. The brief is already written: short, modern, consumer, tied to the product action; no poetic or outdoorsy names, nothing TikTok-derived, no -It construction, no implementation vocabulary.

Recommendation: Kept. I first proposed Landed, because the product's signature moment is pins landing. The product lead argued for Kept and I think it is right, on the test this brand actually has to pass: the failure screen. At the measured hit rate, the most-seen sentence in the product is the one on the no-places screen. “Nothing to keep from this post” is matter-of-fact and offers a next move. Landed has no equivalent sentence. Kept also names the relationship rather than the mechanic — every other candidate describes a pin.

Alternatives, in order: Pindrop if you weight ownability over meaning · Spots, the word the code already uses · Landed · Stash. Of the four already explored, Pinned ranks highest and pays for it in Pinterest adjacency. Trademark and domain are unverified for every one of these.

2. The rail — the honesty ruling

The progress rail is client-side, and I want to be precise about how: it is not faked with timers. The code is scrupulous — it marks the first stage done the moment the fetch is issued, because that is the only boundary it can honestly report. The result is one tick and one spinner for seven to thirty-four seconds.

Ruling I'd like you to confirm: the facelift may not ship a more convincing fake. The middle path is real and it is the highest-value change in the whole audit — split the source fetch into its own request. oEmbed and the caption return in under a second; extraction takes the rest. Two round trips, still request/response, no streaming route. The post appears in about 500 ms and the wait becomes about something. The streaming route stays a real feature on the plan of record, not something the facelift funds to make a demo better.

3. Is the pin allowed to change colour?

Re-tuning the category palette changes every pin in every screenshot, every doc and every existing map. It is the right change — two of the four current colours are indistinguishable at pin size — but it is visible and not free to reverse.

Recommendation: yes, in stage 1. Colour is the map's only encoded fact and it currently doesn't encode. Doing it first means everything after is built on the final palette.

4. Dark mode: a signed pass, or no toggle at all

There is no third option, and that is the useful part of this decision. The .dark block is an abandoned light-blue exploration that nothing in the app ever applies, three modules read prefers-color-scheme independently and would disagree with it, and the map style is light-only by construction. Shipping a toggle onto what exists ships the rejected palette.

Recommendation: the full pass, in stage 4. A night map is the largest unused visual asset this product has. But it is a feature-sized piece of work, not a value swap, and it is honest to schedule it as one.

5. Can the collections index become a card grid?

ux-collections.md §1.6 rules out a grid, and the reasoning was sound at the time: a grid of covers with no covers is worse than a list. The proposal here is a generated cover built from the collection's own data — a low-zoom map fragment of its real bounding box, with its category dots plotted where the places actually are. Not decoration; the collection itself.

Recommendation: yes, and it reopens a decision rather than ignoring one. With real covers the original argument dissolves. Flagging it as your ruling rather than quietly overriding a ratified document.

Not asking: the typeface

"Manrope only" is signed off and the family has the weight range to carry both roles. A second display face is the fastest way to stop looking like a well-built default, and I still think it is the smaller lever.

Proceeding on: keep Manrope, add the missing type scale, and revisit once you have seen stages 1 and 3. Say the word if you'd rather explore a display face now.

Diagnosis

It is not thin on features. It is thin on places.

Every emptiness downstream is that one fact wearing a different coat. Here is the arithmetic of a first session, measured rather than guessed — and it is the reason no amount of polish was going to fix the feeling.

The spread is not sloppiness, it is the shape of the data. The measured sample yielded ten places from three successful posts, distributed 1, 1, 8 — one caption supplies eighty per cent of the places in the whole sample. Take the median hit and it is nineteen pastes to five places; take the mean and it is six. With n=3 hits, neither end is solid. What survives every model is the second number: roughly thirteen of every eighteen pastes end on the no-places screen, and that barely moves whichever yield you assume.

At three places, everything we built is inert

Search, filters, near me, been / not been yet — every retrieval feature is correct and useless without density. The map is worse than a note until roughly place fifteen. We designed for the thirty-place case and shipped to the three-place one.

The library arrives in bursts, not a trickle

A listicle adds three to seven places in one city on one day, then nothing for a week. That shape breaks different things than a steady stream does — and it breaks them sooner. See Bursts.

The strongest argument in the whole exercise, and it is emotional rather than arithmetic

Eighteen sequential paste–wait–confirm cycles with thirteen failure screens reads as a broken product. The identical twenty-seven per cent inside a queue — a hundred links in, twenty-seven places out, the failures summarised as one line — reads as a success. Nothing about the extraction changed. Bulk import does not merely raise places per interaction; it hides a failure rate we cannot yet fix. That is a product argument, not an integration one, and it is why the input work outranks the feature work.

Defects

Four found while looking. All verified in the code.

These were not on any list. They came out of asking six specialists what a person actually gets, and each one was confirmed against the source before it was written down.

G1

The first screen was designed, specified, and never built

showImport is useState(false). ux-map-is-the-query.md §5 specifies the empty-library screen — a regional map framed from the browser timezone with the import overlay already open — and says it needs “no new components and no permission prompt”. Line 6 of that same document admits the overlay is unbuilt. What a new user actually sees is one sentence and a plus button.

Ship first
G2

A post naming thirteen places yields zero places, not twelve

ExtractionEnvelopeSchema caps candidates at 12. safeParse fails the whole envelope, and the item-by-item salvage runs only after the envelope parses. Above the cap the behaviour is a cliff, not a truncation — and “12 Best Coffee Shops in Melbourne” is an entirely ordinary caption format. The 12 exists as a flood guard against a model emitting forty hashtags as places; a higher envelope bound that truncates to the candidate cap keeps the guard and removes the cliff.

Correctness
G3

Our best real listicle loses its last place by one

The exploringlondon post names eight venues. MAX_CANDIDATES = 7. The eighth is extracted, never resolved, and shown as capped. GEMINI_MAX_CANDIDATES = 8 caps the model's own response schema too, so there is no headroom to widen into.

Off by one
G4

An Instagram link becomes a place name

universal-input.ts classifies any non-TikTok URL as kind: 'text', which manual add then treats as a name — so the Add sheet offers Add “https://www.instagram.com/reel/D…” manually. The /import route handles the identical URL correctly, with its own screen. Two surfaces, opposite behaviours.

Correctness
G5

The candidate the resolver never saw is the one that saves silently

A capped candidate is correctly excluded from the resolver chip and from the pin-provenance line — the code is explicit that “we never looked” is not “we looked and found nothing”. But willSave returns effectivePick(…) !== null || modelHasCoordinates, and a capped candidate has no pick — so whenever the model produced coordinates it arrives pre-ticked and saves a model-guessed pin. The card on that screen carrying the least information about where its pin came from is the one nothing ever verified.

Next

Walk radius from a chosen origin

Near me sorts; it does not filter. The hundred places you cannot walk to are still rows beneath the ones you can. Needs haversineKm, a fourth term in the existing AND chain and a circle on the map — no provider, no stored data. It is also the only control that cuts through a burst of seven same-day, same-city, all-unvisited saves, because distance is the one dimension a batch write does not flatten.

Global search

Search is bound to the viewport, so a user who knows the name but not the city has to move the camera before they can find their own place.

/p/[id] — a shareable place

Every share today requires creating a collection first. This would be the product's only outward-facing surface. Needs an RLS read policy and a privacy ruling on what a link exposes.

The multi-source place

saved_place_sources is keyed (saved_place_id, source_id), so one place can carry many TikToks — Charter invariant 4, in the schema. The detail renders exactly one.

A destination surface for a city

Promote the existing area row into a real target that frames the city, scopes the list and says 4 not been yet. Half this product's stated value is a trip not yet taken.

A tag facet with counts

So the library finally has a self-portrait, and a tag is discoverable without stumbling across it on a place.

Refused & blocked

What we are not building, and what we cannot.

Blocked, not declined

“Open now” is the single most-wanted question on a saved-restaurants map, and we cannot answer it: we resolve through Google, and 06 §3.1 forbids pairing Google content with a non-Google map. That needs a licensing answer before it is a design question.

A price filter is the right idea from the wrong source — only 2 of 16 captions carry price. The real source is the resolver field mask, which requests five fields and not priceLevel.

The PWA share target is Android only and unavailable on iOS, requires the user to install the app first, and delivers 0.63 places per interaction — so on the places-per-interaction test it is the least defensible of the build-worthy paths. The repo's own capture evidence reached the same verdict by another route: a polish item wearing an adoption item's clothes. Instagram is unavailable; YouTube is assumed possible and unproven.

Refused, so nobody re-proposes them

Itinerary and route planning. Ratings and scores. A social graph, public profiles or a discovery feed. Streaks, badges and gamification. AI chat. And Google Takeout import — which would fill a map fast and turn the product into a Maps mirror rather than a map of what your feed recommended, which is the entire positioning.

The one thing

Ship the zero-state. It is already designed, it needs no new components, it costs about a day, and it is the exact moment the product feels like an empty demo — because at that moment it literally is one.
