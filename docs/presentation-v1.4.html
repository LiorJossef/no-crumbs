01 · The problem

You are standing on the street. The bakery is 400 videos down.

Saving is instant. Getting one back is not. A saved folder is ordered by the moment you tapped it — so the only question you ever actually ask it, “what did I save near here?”, is the one question it cannot answer.

The realistic outcome is not a slow search. It is no search at all.

Dinner somewhere you never saved. That is the loss — not storage, and not elapsed seconds, but a decision made without the thing you deliberately kept.

02 · Who it is for

One person, asking two questions.

Someone who already saves places on TikTok and cannot get them back. Not two segments — one profile in two moods, at home and away.

Everyday

What did I save around here?

Standing in a neighbourhood, deciding where to eat. Answered by the places nearest you — offered when it pays off, never demanded on first load.

Trips

What do I already have in this city?

Landing somewhere, or planning to. Answered by the whole library on one map, anywhere in the world, with no location permission needed at all.

And who the customer is

The same person. There is nobody else to sell to.

No advertiser, no one buying access to anyone’s data or attention. That is not modesty about the business model — it is what lets the map be private, the ownership live in the database, and your live location never be kept on our side.

The value is plain: the collecting half of TikTok already works, the retrieval half does not, and this is the retrieval half.

05 · Architecture

The model can name a place. It cannot place it.

That sentence is the whole architecture. Reading a caption and knowing where a restaurant stands are two different jobs, and the second one is a directory lookup.

The callCoordinates from the model measured 65 to 470 m out, and the screen had to admit it: “Pin is approximate.” On a map product, the pin being wrong is the product being wrong — so the next thing built was not a feature, it was matching.
The ruleThe domain layer has no outgoing edge. It cannot reach the network or the database, so the rules stay testable on their own — and a test, not a convention, holds the line.

06 · The honest outcome

“No places in this one” is the usual answer.

Plenty of videos name no place anyone could find. That is not a bug to hide behind a spinner — it is the outcome to design for.

So it was built as somewhere to land, not as an error.

The video, the handle, the caption we read, and a way to add a place by name instead — one tap away, in the same screen you are already in.

Never fakedAdding a link is one request and one response, with no percentage to report — so nothing on screen fills, counts down, or completes. It says what it is doing, and nothing more.
Never guessedAn extracted name and an inferred one are kept apart all the way down. Where the product is unsure it asks, and where it cannot know it says so.
Not a failureA link from another platform is a recognised redirect to adding by hand, not an error message. We support one platform well rather than four badly.

07 · The database

One row per place. One row per person who saved it.

Privacy here is not a screen remembering to check. It is held in the database, on every table, under a policy forced even for the table’s owner — so a query that forgets its filter returns nothing rather than everything.

17

tables, with row-level security enabled and forced on every one

38

policies, each one proved by making it fail first

0

grants to the anonymous role. None, anywhere

places — shared

The place itself: name, category, coordinates, address. One row per real place, de-duplicated, so two people who save the same café point at the same row rather than at two copies of it.

saved_places — private

Your relationship to that place: your note, your tags, the video you added it from. Unique on (user_id, place_id), so saving the same place twice updates one row instead of growing your map.

On text we did not write

A caption is words to read, never instructions to follow.

The words belong to a stranger. They are treated that way at every boundary they cross, including the one into the model.

Proved277 assertions across 8 suites, run against a real database with two real accounts.
The keyThe privileged database key never reaches the browser, and every route identifies the person before it does anything.

09 · Scale

The thing that runs out first is an invoice.

Not the database, and not the map. Every read is scoped to one person at the index, so a hundredth account adds rows that no query walks. What actually runs out is a hundred directory lookups a day.

Grows withLinks added, not people signed up. Adding one link is three outside calls; a new account on its own costs nothing to serve.
Your mapArrives in one read, whole. The drawing is bounded, not the reading — which is why the list, the pins and search can never disagree with each other.
Pins landingEight waves, 60 ms apart, from the middle of your library outward — because the middle is where you are already looking.
No queueBy decision. No job outlives its request, so the tables a queue would have needed already exist as caches inside the database.

09 · Scale, continued

Where it bends, and what it costs to fix.

Three walls, in the order we would actually hit them — each one measured, not guessed.

NowA hundred lookups a day, across everyone. Raising it is a billing decision; the engineering that goes with it is one per-person cap, in the same change.
SoonPlace names appear in tiers as you zoom rather than all at once. Turning them all on at two thousand pins costs roughly half the frame budget — you would feel it in your thumb.
LaterA library in the high hundreds wants a paged read. The index it needs is already in the schema; today the query cannot use it, because a read with no limit fetches everything anyway.

None of that is the database struggling at hundreds of people. The architecture is not what limits this product today — and knowing which wall you are actually walking towards is most of the work.

10 · Testing

Every control is proved by making it fail first.

A policy test that only ever passes proves nothing — it might be asking an empty table. Each one here denies before it allows, so green means the rule fired, not that nothing happened.

Level 1Unit and domain — 251 files, no network, no database. Component render sits here too: it runs in the same runner, against a static string.
Level 2In a browser — 13 specifications, phone and desktop.
Level 3Database policy — two real people, and the attempt by one to read the other.
Level 4By hand — including the link on slide 04, added in a browser and read back in psql.

One test, and the person it is for

On your map, colour is the whole encoding. So a test keeps two categories apart.

Restaurant Café Bar Not known yet

The café amber as first drawn sat too near the restaurant persimmon. On screen they were two colours; under the commonest colour blindness — about one man in twelve — they collapsed into one. The shipped inks were re-picked apart, and a test over the two ink values fails the pair we drew first.

11 · What comes next

In order, and for a stated reason.

Not a wish list. Each one answers a limit that has already been measured, sequenced by the condition that triggers it.

FirstRaise the lookup quota, and cap each person daily in the same change — so one account cannot spend everyone’s allowance.
ThenStream the add. The one wait in the product is 7 to 34 seconds and today it is a single request — the caption could arrive first, and the places as they are matched.
The real prizeRead more than the caption. A video can recommend a place without ever typing its name — on screen, or out loud. That is the ceiling on what the product can find today.
At scalePage your map once a library passes a few hundred places, and count collections instead of loading them.

The rule every one of them is held to

No change may increase what the product asserts.
