What this is, in six lines

People find restaurants on TikTok and save the video. TikTok stores that as a video in a date‑ordered list — not a place, not a location, not a name. So saving works and getting it back out is broken.

No Crumbs takes one TikTok link, reads the post on the server, pulls out the places, matches them to real coordinates, and puts them on your own private map.

The honest headline: not every video names its place in words. Plenty say it out loud or show it on screen instead, and we read captions, not audio or frames. So “no places in this one” is a normal outcome rather than an error — which is why it is a designed screen with three ways forward.

If you read one section

The four things this project got right

The course grades quality of thinking over quantity of features. These are the decisions we would defend in an interview.

01

Permission is enforced by the database, not by application code

Row‑Level Security is enabled and forced on all 17 tables, and users hold no write grant on any shared table. We did not describe this and hope — we attacked it, as a second real account and as an anonymous visitor, and every one of seventeen attempts returned zero rows or was denied.

proved
02

The browser cannot state a fact about a place

When you save, it sends an index and your own note — never a name or a coordinate. Every value reaching the database is re‑derived on the server. That came from a real bug: because place rows are shared, an earlier version let one request relocate a venue other people had saved.

designed
03

The architecture is checked, not just described

The domain layer depends on nothing, and a linter enforces it on every build. Any module holding a secret imports server-only, so a key that would reach the browser fails the build rather than shipping quietly.

enforced
04

The most common outcome is a designed screen

Not every video names its place in words. Rather than treat that as an error path, “no places in this one” is a first‑class screen with three ways forward — it never blames the user and never asks them to paste a caption, because the moment we ask for typed text the product has no reason to exist.

product

Product Specification

What problem No Crumbs solves, who it is for, who the customer is, what the business goals are, what the software has to do — and the core processes it lets people perform.

The problem Users Customer Business goals Capabilities Core processes The boundary

One line

People save the video. What they wanted was the place.

No Crumbs turns the places you find on TikTok into pins on your own private map, so you can find them again by where they are instead of when you saved them.

People find restaurants, cafés and bars on TikTok, and they save the video. But TikTok stores that save as a video in a reverse‑chronological list. It does not store where the place is, what kind of place it is, or what it is called.

So collecting works and getting things back out is broken — and getting things back out is the only moment that matters:

  • “I’m in this neighbourhood right now — what did I save near me?”
  • “I land in Tokyo on Thursday — what do I already have there?”
  • “I want dinner tonight and I don’t want to scroll.”

A saved‑list thumbnail rarely shows the name or the city, so answering “what did I save near me” means opening posts one by one. Checking fifteen of them costs a few minutes of real effort — and the usual outcome is worse than slow: the person gives up and goes somewhere they never saved. The recommendation they deliberately kept had no effect on the decision. That is the loss.

What is deliberately not the problem

Discovery. No Crumbs never suggests a place you did not save. No feed, no ranking, no “places you might like”. You already have too many recommendations — what you cannot do is reach the ones you chose.

On the cost figures

The “few minutes” above is an estimate, not a measurement. A timed baseline was planned and never run. It stays labelled as an estimate rather than being quietly promoted to a result.

M2 · who the users are

One person, two retrieval questions

Everyday

They save places in the city they live in, and want to know what is saved near them right now.

Trips

They save in bursts before travelling, and want to know what they already have in a city they do not know yet.

Two facts that shape everything

This happens on a phone, standing up, mid‑session. So the design is mobile‑first — bottom sheets and thumb reach, not a shrunk‑down desktop page.

Importing is the cost, retrieval is the reward. Every tap in the import flow is paid before any value is received, which is why the quality budget is spent there.

Who it is not for

Stated so the boundary can be tested: people looking for new places to go; itinerary planners who want day‑by‑day routing and bookings; creators or businesses wanting an audience; and anyone with fewer than about ten saved places, where a plain list still beats a map.

M2 · who the customer is

The user and the customer are the same person

There is no advertiser, no business account, and no third party buying access to anyone’s data or attention. The product’s only obligation is to the person whose map it is.

That is also why the security posture is strict: a private map, ownership enforced in the database rather than in application code, and the user’s live location never stored on the server.

No pricing is proposed and nothing in this version was designed to make money. If it ever were, the obvious model is a paid tier for the highest‑volume savers, because they feel the pain most.

M2 · core processes

The flagship: a TikTok link becomes pins on your map

01

Paste

You copy a link in TikTok, switch to No Crumbs and paste it into the one field. We check and canonicalise it; anything that is not a TikTok link is rejected immediately and by name.

Client
02

Accept

We confirm within about a second and start reading. The import is signed‑in and rate‑limited, with a hard ceiling on how many provider calls one import may make.

Server
03

Read the caption

Fetched from TikTok’s public embed endpoint. The caption is treated as untrusted data, never as instructions.

Server
04

Extract, then resolve

Candidate places come out of the model; each is resolved to a real venue with coordinates. Confidence comes from the resolution evidence — name similarity, city agreement, category agreement, and how far ahead the winner is — never from the model’s opinion of itself.

Server
05

Review

Confident matches are pre‑selected; unconfident ones are offered as a choice. Nothing has been saved yet.

Client
06

Confirm

The places land on your map, de‑duplicated, each keeping its source link.

Write
07

Retrieve, later

You open the app somewhere and the map answers the question. This is the step the other six exist for.

The point

Secondary processes

Find what is near you · browse, search and filter the list · open a place and jump back to the TikTok video that recommended it · add a place by name · mark a place as been · put places into a collection and share it with a named person · sign up, sign in, and delete your account.

Not a process, ever

Typing or pasting the text of a post. It does not exist in the product and no flow leads to it.

M2 · the boundary

Three decisions, and the limitation we lead with

One link, in one field

No uploads, no screenshots, no share‑sheet integration — a web app cannot appear in the iOS share sheet, and designing a flow that cannot exist is worse than not having it.

TikTok only

The one platform we verified we can read from a server with no key and no account — 16 of 16 real public posts, about 0.6 s at the 90th percentile. An Instagram or YouTube link is recognised by name and sent to “add this place by name”: a redirect, not a failure.

What we store is fixed

Name, category, coordinates, the link back to the post, and your own note. No hours, photos, ratings or reviews — because what we chose to keep is exactly what open data lets us keep forever.

The honest limitation, stated rather than buried

We read captions, not audio or video frames. A great many TikToks name the place only on screen or only out loud, and those we cannot read. Extending to audio or frames is out of scope for this version.

So “no places in this one” is a normal outcome, not an error. We designed for it rather than treating it as an exception: it is a first‑class screen with three ways forward, it never blames the user, and it never asks for the caption.

We are not quoting a hit‑rate percentage. The only figure we ever measured came from an eleven‑post feasibility sample taken before the extraction and resolution work existed, and it was never re‑measured against the finished product. A number that stale is worse than no number.

The one design choice to understand

The split between places and saved_places. Venue facts are shared; everything a user writes lands on their own row. That is what makes a shared table safe — and it is why one physical place is one row no matter how many videos pointed at it.

M3 · which users and permissions exist

Authorisation is the database, not the application

Two roles, then a third inside collections

An anonymous visitor sees only the landing page and sign‑in. A signed‑in user sees exactly their own library.

Collections add a narrower second model on top: a collection has an owner, and other accounts join as editors or viewers.

How it is enforced

Row‑Level Security is enabled and forced on all 17 tables. anon holds no grant on anything and is named by no policy. Per‑user tables filter on auth.uid(). Shared tables are membership‑gated: you can read a places row only if you have a saved_places row pointing at it, or it sits in a collection you belong to.

Users hold no write grant on shared tables

No INSERT, UPDATE or DELETE on places or sources. Those writes go through database functions. saved_places uses column‑level grants as well, so “give my save to someone else” is not expressible before any policy is even consulted.

Proved, not asserted

Eight SQL policy‑test files carrying 293 assertions run a second real user against the first user’s rows and assert nothing comes back — and assert the positive half first, because “B sees zero” is also satisfied by a database where nobody can read anything.

M3 · pages, routes and actions

Pages, and the three ways the client talks to the server

Pages

/ · /sign-in and the password‑reset pair · /map, which is the product — pins, the saved‑places drawer, search, filters, place detail and collections all sit over one map instance, created once and never destroyed · /import · /collections and a join‑by‑link page · /profile · /account · /healthz, which reports the deployed commit.

The split, and why

A long read‑and‑compute is an API route. A short authenticated mutation is a server action. An ordinary page read is neither — the Server Component queries Postgres directly under the user’s own token, because RLS is already the authorisation layer, so a REST endpoint in front of it would add a second place to get permissions wrong.

M3 · how data flows

Front end, back end, database

Import is two round trips, and that is an honesty decision about time: TikTok answers in under a second, the model takes 7 to 34. So the app shows you the post it just read while the slow part runs underneath.

The trust boundary, and the bug that taught us to draw it here

When you tap Save, the browser sends an extraction id, a candidate index, an option index and your own note — nothing else. Every fact that reaches the database is re‑derived on the server from the row the server itself wrote.

That is not tidiness. An earlier version took the name and coordinates from the request, and because places rows are shared, that let one request rename and relocate a venue other people had saved.

M4 · central business logic

The pipeline’s asymmetry is the design

Reading the post can fail the import

It is the only stage that can. With no text there is nothing to show, so this is the one hard stop.

Resolution never fails it

An unresolved name degrades to a row you can fix yourself. Eight unresolved names beat a failure screen.

Finding nothing is a success

Because it is a common outcome, not a rare one — plenty of captions name no venue at all. It gets its own screen with two ways forward: type the name, or open the video.

M4 · state, errors, validation

Three things that keep the app predictable

State — no client state library

Persisted data lives in Postgres and is read in Server Components. Which drawer is open lives in the URL. The map and camera live in a ref created once. Transient things live in the component that owns them.

After a mutation the action revalidates and the server re‑renders, so there is no client cache to keep in sync.

Errors — a closed list of 13

Adapters catch every vendor exception and turn it into one of them, so no provider message, status code or stack reaches the browser: the wire carries a code and two booleans, and the wording lives in one file on the client.

Each code has one constructor with its retryable flag baked in, and maps to one HTTP status — with 500 reserved for our own bugs.

Validation — the URL is a security gate

The pasted URL goes through a pure canonicaliser that is also the SSRF gate: exact matching against six TikTok hosts (never a suffix, so tiktok.com.evil.io fails), no IP literals, no explicit ports, and the same check re‑applied to every redirect hop. It runs again on the server even though the browser already ran it, because only the server check counts.

The model’s output is additionally checked against the caption — a venue name with no supporting text is dropped — which is the real defence against a hostile caption.

M6 · defining “working”

The suite is built around what we think will go wrong

Most likely failure

Not a crash — a confident wrong answer. A place saved under the wrong name, at the wrong coordinates, from a caption that never said so.

Most serious failure

A permission failure. Every map is private and there is more than one user. A wrong pin is a disappointment; another user’s pin is a breach.

Most common outcome

“No places found” is a routine result, not a rare one. So the empty screen is a main surface with its own tests, not an error path.

The rule for choosing a layer: a test belongs at the cheapest layer that can still fail for the real reason. And we set no coverage percentage, on purpose — a percentage target pushes effort toward whatever is cheapest to cover, which is never the seam that breaks.

The browser tier has known failures, and we diagnosed every one of them

None of them is a product defect. We traced all four clusters rather than leaving them as noise, and the causes are in the tests:

  • One file inherited the default 30‑second timeout while every sibling raises it, so its sign‑in step ran out of time under parallel load. Fixed — one line.
  • One asserts a navigation control that was deliberately deleted, and contradicts its own preceding assertion. A stale block left behind by a change that was completed.
  • One counts an h1 that is hidden at mobile width but still in the document. The product is right; the assertion is breakpoint‑naive.
  • The import test is the interesting one. It fails because the app worked: the demo user had already saved that place from that same video, so the product recognised the duplicate and deselected the candidate — leaving no Save button for the spec to click. The screen it produced was correct. The test assumes a first‑ever import of a fixed URL, and runs twice in parallel at two viewports as the same user.

Being able to say why a test is red is the point of having tests. A suite that is green because it asserts too little would be worth less.

M6 · mandatory for us

How permissions are tested

Authorisation lives in the database, so we prove it in the database, as two different real users.

The design point worth stating

“User B reads zero of user A’s rows” is satisfied just as well by a database where nobody can read anything. So every file asserts the positive half first — A reading exactly their own rows — before any “B sees zero” assertion is reached.

The files also test forgery, not just reading. B cannot write a provenance row carrying A’s user id. A collection editor cannot add a place from someone else’s library. added_by cannot be faked. An unknown, a revoked and an expired invite token all fail identically, so a token cannot be probed for existence. A collection owner cannot have their own membership deleted or demoted.

Above that, saved-places.test.ts checks that every mutation refuses without a session and never reaches the database, and account-deletion.test.ts checks that deleting your account takes no user id as a parameter — a delete endpoint that accepts a user id is a delete endpoint that can delete somebody else.

Honesty

What we do not test, and what our tests cannot tell you

Deliberately not tested

The model’s answers — only their shape, and whether they are grounded in the caption. Pinning “this caption gives this venue” would pin a third party’s non‑deterministic behaviour and break on a model update that was an improvement.

Live third‑party calls — money, flakiness, and a test that goes red when a vendor is down tells you nothing about your code.

Screenshot comparison — a product mid‑redesign would have its baseline re‑approved on nearly every commit, which is the same as having none while paying for it. And no frame‑rate gate: a flake generator on shared hardware.

What our tests cannot prove

105 of 250 unit files assert against source text. That is a legitimate way to prove something is absent, but it proves the code says something, not that the running app does it.

No component test is evidence about layout, size or contrast, because a static HTML string has no CSS.

No automated test here can tell you the map opens somewhere useful. That is why our working agreement requires running the real app and reading the saved rows before anything is called done.

M8 · tens and hundreds of users

No screen ever searches a shared table

Some tables grow with the number of users — your saves, imports and collections. Others are shared by everyone and grow with the product — place identities, TikTok posts, cached provider answers.

The thing that matters is that every read starts from a row that belongs to the person asking, and reaches shared data through it. So one user’s cost does not depend on how many other users exist.

At two hundred people each pasting forty links, that is under fifty thousand rows in total. Postgres does not notice a database that size. We checked the claim rather than assuming it: we ran the real queries against a synthetic 300,000‑row database and read the query plans back. The user’s own library was found by an index every time, and other users’ rows were never touched.

M8 · indexes & over-fetching

Chosen, not sprinkled

What we index, and why

Saved places carry three: “this user, newest first”, “does this user already have this place”, and “who has saved this place” — the last is what makes the security rules cheap. Collections index membership and item order. Place search uses trigrams, which is what makes fuzzy name matching fast: measured about 24× faster on real data.

We also found two indexes that serve nothing — one on tags (filtering happens in the browser) and one on coordinates (nothing queries by coordinate). We documented them rather than quietly deleting them. And one that is genuinely missing: the invites table has no index on the collection it belongs to. Not needed today, needed later — saying that is more useful than adding it blind.

How over‑fetching is avoided

We never write select *. Every query lists its columns — and the database enforces it from its own side: the app’s login is granted permission only on the specific columns it may read, so the TikTok caption text physically cannot reach a browser even if someone asked for it.

Sharing a collection sends a collaborator a place’s name and location and nothing about you — not your note, not your tags, not whether you have been.

M8 · pagination & the client/server split

Two answers, one of them a limitation

Pagination: there is none, and that is real

The map fetches the whole library every time. On purpose: the map must draw a camera box around all your places on first paint, the list beside it has to agree with the pins, and search has to find a place currently off screen. All three need the whole set.

Measured cost: about 1.3 KB per saved place — so 100 places is ~180 KB and 1,000 would approach a megabyte. We also measured what pagination would buy: a limit of fifty changes the plan, uses an index we already have, removes the sort entirely, and does about 2.5× less work. The fix is ready. We just do not need it yet.

The client/server split holds

Every page fetches its data on the server, signed in as the user, so the database’s own rules apply to every read. Every change goes through a server action. The browser holds interaction only — and does not talk to the database at all.

Search, category filters, tag chips and been/not‑been all run in the browser over the array the page already sent. Faster than a round trip at our sizes, and it means the pins and the list cannot disagree, because they are the same array. We checked the alternative: searching on the server was slower, because a “contains this text” search cannot use an index anyway.

M8 · current limitations

Where it breaks first — and it is not the database

The honest answer to “can this handle a hundred users?”

Our Google Places project is capped at 100 place lookups per day for everyone combined, and one import can spend up to eight. That is somewhere between 12 and 50 imports a day for the whole product. At a hundred users that is well under one import per person per day.

The database can take a hundred users. The budget cannot. It is a billing setting, not a code problem — but it is the real ceiling.

Two more shared ceilings

The model allowance is 500 calls a day, and natural‑language search spends one every time someone submits a sentence — so search competes with importing for the same budget, and nothing in the code counts what has been spent.

The import is one long request

Fetch the post, call the model, look up as many as eight places one after another — all inside a single web request, with no maximum duration set or measured. A slow chain fails with nothing saved.

The limiters are in memory

The two rate limiters we do have reset on deploy and do not span server instances. There is no caching on the read path at all, and the profile page reads the library twice in one render.

M9 · authentication

Who you are

How sign‑in works

Supabase Auth, email and password. It issues a signed token held in a cookie. A small piece of middleware refreshes that session on every page request, and /map refuses to render without one.

We wrote no password handling of our own — which is the point. Hashing, reset tokens and session rotation are the parts most likely to be got subtly wrong.

What the token then does

Every server‑side read runs as that user, not as an admin. The user’s id is available inside the database as auth.uid(), which is what lets the database itself decide what they may see.

M9 · authorisation & restricted actions

Permission is a database rule, not an if statement

This is the single most important decision in the document. If permission is checked in application code, then every new page, route and action is a fresh chance to forget it. Ours is checked by Postgres, underneath all of them.

The rules

Row‑Level Security is enabled and forced on all 17 tables. The anonymous role holds no grant on anything and is named by no policy. Per‑user tables filter on auth.uid(). Shared tables are membership‑gated — you can read a place only if you saved it, or it is in a collection you belong to.

Writes are narrower still

Users hold no insert, update or delete grant on any shared table; those writes go through database functions. Saved places use column‑level grants, so “give my save to someone else” is not expressible before any policy is consulted.

Restricted to the signed‑in user

Everything except the landing page, sign‑in and the health endpoint. Importing, saving, editing, deleting, collections and account deletion all verify the session first and refuse without one. Account deletion takes no user id as a parameter — a delete endpoint that accepts one is a delete endpoint that can delete somebody else.

M9 · API protection & input validation

Two untrusted inputs, handled differently

Every route checks the session first

All six application API routes verify the session before doing any work; only the health endpoint and the auth callback are public. Server actions do the same.

The strongest of them is the confirm step: the browser sends an index, never a place fact. Every value that reaches the database is re‑derived on the server from a row the server itself wrote.

The pasted link is a security boundary

It becomes an outbound HTTP request, so it is the SSRF gate: exact matching against six TikTok hosts — never a suffix, so tiktok.com.evil.io fails — no IP literals, no explicit ports, and the same check re‑applied to every redirect hop. It runs again on the server even though the browser already ran it.

The caption is data, never instructions, and the model’s output is checked back against the caption: a venue name with no supporting text is dropped. That grounding check is the real defence against a hostile caption.

M9 · secrets

Where keys live

Secrets exist only in Vercel’s encrypted store and in an untracked local file. The repository permits exactly one environment file, the example with no values in it. Rotating a key is a store edit plus a redeploy — no code references a literal value.

The enforcement is a build‑time one: any module holding a secret imports server-only, so a key that would reach the browser bundle fails the build rather than shipping quietly. The one public key, the Supabase anon key, is public by design — access is controlled by row‑level security, not by hiding it.

M9 · which risks remain

What we know is still open

No exploitable cross‑user vulnerability was found. These are the things we decided to live with, and why.

01

No rate limit on the import route

A signed‑up account could loop it, spending one paid model call per request at our expense. The fix is a per‑user counter over a table and index that already exist. Accepted because sign‑up is required and the population is the owner plus an examiner — the first public sign‑up link changes that.

medium
02

No content‑security policy, and the session cookie is not HttpOnly

An attacker would need an XSS first, and there is no injection path: React escapes by default and no user‑authored or third‑party HTML is rendered anywhere. Accepted because a half‑policy that gets loosened on its first failure is worse than none.

medium
03

Captions are kept indefinitely, with no deletion path

A caption and handle are a third party’s personal data, and nothing in the code ever deletes one. This is a legal gap, not a technical one: we hold published public speech with no stated retention position, at a scale of one user.

medium
04

Playing a TikTok in‑app hands TikTok a long‑lived identifier

Pressing Play here mounts TikTok’s player, which sets a roughly one‑year cross‑site cookie and loads a large fingerprinting script — on a page that also shows where the user has decided to go. Accepted because it is user‑triggered, refusing it is a measured zero‑request path, and the choice is reversible. Sandboxing does not mitigate it; only not pressing it does.

medium
05

Passwords: six characters, no breach check, no lockout of our own

The identity provider’s default. This is the cheapest real improvement available — two lines of configuration.

medium
06

The permission tests are a snapshot, not a gate

They pass, and they do not run on every push — a cost decision, not an oversight: a full run costs about twenty minutes against a two‑thousand‑minute monthly allowance, so the suite runs on pull requests rather than on every commit. “We have RLS tests” and “our RLS tests run on every push” are different claims, and we make only the first.

confidence
07

Four tables would expose a new column by default

They combine a table‑level read grant with a policy that can return another user’s row. No such column exists today — the risk arrives with the next migration that adds one to any of the four.

latent
08

Environments are far apart

Local, staging and production are at different migration heads. Nothing in production is weakened — later controls simply do not exist there yet — but it means some evidence cannot be reproduced on every machine.
